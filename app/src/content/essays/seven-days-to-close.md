Customers Bancorp told investors it closed commercial loans in seven days, against the 30 to 60 days it says is typical.

The claim sits in the bank's [second-quarter earnings release](https://www.customersbank.com/investor-relations/press-releases/press-release/Customers-Bancorp-Reports-Results-for-Second-Quarter-2026-1786/), attached to an [8-K furnished on 23 July](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1488813/000148881326000082/). CEO Sam Sidhu described "a pilot of our new AI-powered loan closing process, which included successfully closing selected commercial loans in seven days, down from 30 to 60 days typically." On the earnings call he called it "a multi-agentic credit underwriting process," said the pilot closed C&I and commercial real estate loans inside a week, and put an 85% reduction on readiness to close. He added 46,000 hours saved through workflow automation, which he valued at 24 full-time staff.

Credit where it's due. Most companies discuss their AI in adjectives, which cost nothing and commit nobody. Sidhu attached numbers to a named process and said them on the record, in documents lawyers read; I'd rather have a number I can argue with than a paragraph I can't.

Then an analyst from JP Morgan asked whether he could quantify the expense savings so far. Sidhu said the technology was "only about six months old," and that this kind of transformation takes "quarters not weeks."

The bank could tell you how much faster the process got. It could not yet tell you what the speed was worth.

This isn't really a Customers Bancorp problem. In lending, speed measures the process, and the result is whether the loans get paid back: a question a loan closed in July 2026 cannot answer yet, though some of it surfaces inside 12 to 18 months in early-payment defaults and covenant breaches, and the rest waits for a downturn. Underwriting is the business of being right about the future. You find out afterwards. That's the shape of the work.

So on the day the seven-day number exists, the number that matters does not. And unless somebody decided in advance to go back and look, it's hard to reconstruct once those loans are mixed in with the rest.

Banks are better placed here than most. [Model risk guidance](https://www.federalreserve.gov/supervisionreg/srletters/sr1107.pdf) has told them to do outcomes analysis since 2011, which is this discipline already written down. Then in April the Fed, OCC and FDIC [replaced that guidance](https://www.federalreserve.gov/supervisionreg/srletters/SR2602.pdf), and the new version says generative and agentic AI are "not within the scope of this guidance." Sidhu's phrase for what his engineers built was "a multi-agentic credit underwriting process."

The Hartford shows you the other version of the same problem. On its second-quarter call, CEO Christopher Swift said underwriting activities were "being completed in a fraction of the time." An analyst pushed for the effect on loss-adjustment expense. Swift talked about "really exciting use cases" and gave no figures. Digital Insurance [reported](https://www.dig-in.com/news/how-ai-is-slashing-underwriting-time-for-the-hartford) that the company "declined to provide specific results."

To be fair, The Hartford has put numbers out elsewhere. At a UBS conference in February it said [75% of small business quotes are processed with no human touch](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/hartford-insurance-group-touts-ai-192318207.html) (a real number, and a flattering one), and that more than 6,000 employees have been trained on its AI tools. This is not a company hiding its AI. It quantified how much it uses AI and stayed quiet about what that produced.

Same pattern as the bank, in a different costume. Adoption is countable this quarter. Outcome is not.

I don't think either company is being dishonest. When a board asks how AI is going, you answer with what you can count by Friday, and what you can count by Friday is nearly always an input: hours, cycle times, seats, quotes touched. Bancorp's 46,000 hours is one of those. The output takes a cycle to show up, and by then whoever announced the project has often moved on.

So before you put AI inside a decision that matters, write down the outcome number, the one that says the decisions were good and not merely quick. For a lender that's delinquency and loss on the loans the tool touched. For a manufacturer running AI visual inspection, it's warranty returns on the batches the system passed. Then do the dull part that makes either of those possible: tag the cases the tool touches at the moment it touches them. A cohort you never marked is one you can't compare later, and that's how most of these reviews quietly die. Name the person who reads the number, and the date it becomes readable. Three lines on one page: the metric, the owner, the date. Do it while you're still excited, because nobody goes hunting for a disappointing number about a project they already announced. That agreement, reached before anyone switches anything on, is a good part of what I do with companies.

None of this argues against closing loans in seven days. Faster is better if the loans are as good, and Customers Bancorp may well have built something very good; they just can't know yet, and neither can you.

Seven days is what a bank can tell you now. Whether it worked is a number that shows up years from now, and only if somebody wrote down that they would go and read it.
