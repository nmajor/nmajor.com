# How a human curation newsletter decides what makes the cut — The Browser (Robert Cottrell / Caroline Crampton / Uri Bram)

- What it is: The most-cited human curation operation — 1,000 candidates/day down to 5 — stated as explicit criteria and triage heuristics. This is the closest thing to a published editorial scoring rubric for a curation newsletter, and it is notable that every criterion is a **durability** test, not a freshness test. ## VERBATIM — the stated selection criteria (ghost.org)

> "Did the editors find it interesting and excellent? Was the writing extraordinary and varied, not just good? Will it be as relevant 10 years from now?"

> "It's pretty easy to find five good pieces every day – honestly you can do it just by picking up the London Review of Books and choosing five pieces at random. What's hard is finding five pieces of writing that will stay with the reader for years to come."

> "So much of the writing that feels 'urgent' in the moment won't be intelligible, let alone relevant, in even a year's time. We want to help our readers find writing of lasting value, pieces that they'll come back to again and again in future."

> "Our target audience is smart, curious people who want to break out of their usual sources and read more widely."

> "Robert and Caroline hold themselves to exceptional standards: if they have five pieces but one of them isn't up to scratch, they'll sit and read for hours more till they find something that's truly Browser-worthy."

Also attributed on that page: Uri Bram noted that curators "underestimate how much time" the process requires; the newsletter aims to provide "variety, surprise, and novelty."

## VERBATIM — the criteria as stated on thebrowser.com (via search result summary; not directly fetched)

> Would we go out of our way to recommend the piece to friends? Will it inform and delight the intelligent general reader? Will it still be worth reading a month or a year from now?

(NOTE: this triad came back through a web-search summary of thebrowser.com, not a direct page fetch. Treat as needing verification against https://thebrowser.com/about/ before quoting publicly.)

## VERBATIM — Cottrell's triage workflow (every.to)

> "I'm able to read a thousand pieces of journalism a day"

> "I'm reading in almost a negative way. I'm triaging."

> "I put quite a lot of trust in headlines, oddly enough, because I figure that headlines are written by loyal allies of the writers"

> "I can tell if something is good if it makes me say: 'I hadn't thought of that', or, 'That's interesting'"

> "the real guarantee of quality in a piece is that the person who's written it is great"

> "At that point the article has to start well. If a piece does not start well then the chances are vanishingly small that it's going to improve."

> "By the end of the day I'll have read to the end, bookmarked, or saved, about 100 articles."

> "from those, I'll select the five that go into the newsletter"

> "five pieces is the number we can reliably fill each day, with pieces that really excite us"

> "I really feel like I've got to be the kind of person that's able to totally stand behind everything that I recommend."

**The implied funnel, stated numerically:** ~1,000 skimmed → ~100 read-to-end/saved → 5 published. A ~10:1 read-through rate and a ~20:1 publish rate from the read pool (200:1 overall).

## VERBATIM/near-verbatim — heuristics from the North Star Podcast notes (podcastnotes.org)

> "The best predictor for the quality of a writing piece? – The writer"

> "The moment you stop enjoying a book, movie, article— whatever it may be—DROP IT"

> "If something doesn't start well, it almost never gets better"

> "Just because an article is new doesn't make it better"

> "The greatest virtue of a writer is honesty"

> "The best articles have just ONE central idea"

Paraphrased-by-the-note-taker (NOT direct quotes — flagged as such):
- Seeks "articles with lasting value" while avoiding pieces that become dated quickly.
- Explicitly avoids breaking news, as "there'll almost always be a more detailed—and better—story about the event the following day or week".
- Reviews "at least 1,000 pieces of writing each day" across a decade (~3 million articles).
- Stops reading immediately upon detecting low quality — sometimes after headlines alone.
- Editorial advice: "Write fewer articles, only focusing on topics they specialize in and care deeply about".
