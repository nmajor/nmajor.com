August 11, 2026 
 
Millions of Americans are struggling to make ends meet and to afford basic necessities.1  
On top of rising health care and housing costs, increasing inflation, and volatile gas prices due to 
Trump’s reckless war of choice, Americans now have to worry their personal data may be used to 
charge them higher prices.2  I am very concerned about companies potentially using Americans’ 
personal data to determine what prices they see and pay, and I am continuing an inquiry into just 
how widespread this practice is.  Consumers deserve to know if businesses are using their 
personal information to manipulate the prices they pay or experiment with algorithms to set the 
prices they see.   
 
While many industries have long used “dynamic pricing” or geographical distinctions to 
justify different costs for the same goods and services in response to changes in supply and 
demand, more and more companies appear to be relying on technological advances to maximize 
profit at the expense of consumers who are already stretched thin financially.3  Now, a growing 
number of companies are implementing or considering “surveillance pricing” where they acquire 
extensive personal data about individual consumers and feed it into algorithms to curate different 
prices dependent on the customer.4  Americans seem completely unaware that the prices they are 
paying, particularly and most notably when shopping online, might have been set using their own 
personal data.5   
 
The airline industry specifically has faced several public allegations of surveillance 
pricing.  One study found that an airline boosted its own revenue by as much as six percent by 
leveraging artificial intelligence (AI) pricing based on consumer information, an increase that 
comes at the expense of consumers.6  Lawsuits have been filed against airlines alleging the use

These actions by companies also exploit the lack of comprehensive consumer privacy 
regulation.9  Existing federal law does not sufficiently protect Americans’ data from misuse or 
adequately address consumer privacy concerns in surveillance pricing.10  In an effort to better 
understand the extent of surveillance pricing in the market, the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) 
conducted a study and in January 2025 released initial findings.11  The study examined eight 
companies and found that consumers’ characteristics and behaviors, like location, browser 
history, demographics, and shopping history, all could be tracked and exploited by retailers to 
tailor consumer pricing.12  These consumer characteristics are compiled from a variety of 
sources, including third-party profiling techniques and inferred data.13  For example, companies 
can infer “aspects such [as] a shopper’s emotional state, purchase intent, or financial sensitivity” 
from actions as small as “placing an item in a cart, but not purchasing, or sorting a feed of 
products from ‘lowest’ to ‘highest’ price” and then use these inferences to set different prices.14  
Former FTC Chair Lina Khan concluded that “[t]he FTC should continue to investigate 
surveillance pricing practices because Americans deserve to know how their private data is being 
used to set the prices they pay and whether firms are charging different people different prices 
for the same good or service.”15  
 
According to a recently released investigation from Consumer Reports and Groundwork 
Collaborative, Instacart allegedly used an AI tool to conduct pricing tests so retailers could gauge 
online shoppers’ reactions to higher or lower prices across a variety of products.16  Instacart

Several states have taken on surveillance pricing concerns by passing new laws to 
regulate the practice.20  In July, New Jersey banned surveillance pricing for groceries and other 
necessities and became the first state to provide customers with a private right of action when 
Governor Sherrill signed the Fair Price Protection Act.21  New York recently passed a state law 
requiring transparency, mandating that businesses display the following disclosure on 
algorithmically set prices: “This price was set by an algorithm using your personal data.”22  In an 
apparent attempt to comply with this new law, Target’s website displays the disclosure at the very 
bottom of the pop-up that appears when customers click on the “i” icon next to the price of an 
item.23  Regardless of whether this meets the “clear and conspicuous” requirement of the law, the 
admission of this form of surveillance pricing is an alarming exposure of potentially harmful 
actions against consumers. 
To better understand the scope of the surveillance pricing ecosystem and information 
companies are collecting, purchasing, using, selling, or aggregating, on May 11, 2026, I sent a 
letter to 25 companies requesting details on their data and pricing practices.  In continuation of 
that inquiry, I am requesting information and answers to the following questions by no later than 
August 25, 2026.  For purposes of responding to these questions, please provide all information 
for the period of time beginning January 1, 2025.  Please format your responses so that every 
answer corresponds to the numbering system below, including a distinct response to each subpart 
question.
