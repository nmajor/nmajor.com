## 1. NO follow-on tribunal/court decision citing Moffatt v. Air Canada

Searched extensively for any court or tribunal decision since Moffatt (2024 BCCRT 149) that
cites it or decides a chatbot-statement case. Result: **none found.**

Everything returned was either (a) the Moffatt decision itself, (b) law-firm commentary on
Moffatt, or (c) academic case comment on Moffatt. The academic pieces located:

- St-Hilaire, I., "Negligent Misrepresentation in Moffatt v Air Canada", UBC Law Review, 2025.
  URL: https://commons.allard.ubc.ca/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1376&context=ubclawreview
  Google Scholar line: "by I St-Hilaire · 2025 · Cited by 7"
  CanLII mirror: https://www.canlii.org/en/commentary/doc/2025CanLIIDocs1963
  Notable line from CanLII snippet: "Had Air Canada's chatbot been powered by an AI system, the
  erroneous response it provided to Moffatt could have been considered as falling within the third [...]"
  ^ This is an important nuance: at least one scholar argues Air Canada's bot may not have been
    a generative AI system at all. Worth flagging if Moffatt is described as an "AI" case.

- "Chatbots and Liability for Negligent Misrepresentation", SAL Practitioner (Singapore Academy
  of Law), case comment on Moffatt.
  URL: https://journalsonline.academypublishing.org.sg/Journals/SAL-Practitioner/Technology/ctl/eFirstSALPDFJournalView/mid/595/ArticleId/2065/Citation/JournalsOnlinePDF

CONCLUSION: Two and a half years on, Moffatt still appears to be the only decided consumer
chatbot-misrepresentation case in a common-law jurisdiction. That absence is itself the story.

## 2. NO regulator enforcement action against a company for what its own customer chatbot said

Checked FTC, US state AGs, UK CMA/FCA, ACCC, Canadian provincial regulators, EU/Italian AGCM.

What EXISTS is adjacent but different:
- FTC's 13+ "AI-washing" cases since 2024 (DoNotPay, Workado, Air AI, Growth Cave, Cox Media
  Group / "Active Listening", Publishing.com). These are about **deceptive claims made in
  marketing ABOUT an AI product** — not about a company's chatbot misleading a customer and
  the company disclaiming it. Different shape. Do not conflate.
  - DLA Piper, 26 May 2026: https://www.dlapiper.com/en-us/insights/publications/2026/05/ftc-ai-washing-action-underscores-enforcement-in-business-to-business-context
    > "On May 21, 2026, the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) announced its latest artificial
    > intelligence (AI)-washing case... By our count, this action is the thirteenth AI-washing
    > case the FTC has filed since 2024."
  - Hunton, 1 June 2026: https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/ftc-announces-settlements-with-three-marketing-firms-over-allegations-of-deceptive-statements-about-active-listening-ai-powered-services
    > "requiring the firms to pay a total of $930,000"

- FTC proposed policy statement, "Policy Statement Concerning the Suppression of Accuracy in
  Artificial Intelligence Systems", Federal Register 7 July 2026, Docket FTC-2026-0859,
  comments due 31 July 2026. Commission vote 2-0.
  URL: https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/07/07/2026-13628/policy-statement-concerning-the-suppression-of-accuracy-in-artificial-intelligence-systems
  FTC press release: https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/07/ftc-seeks-public-comment-policy-statement-addressing-ai-accuracy
  > "In a December executive order, President Trump directed the FTC to issue a policy statement
  > addressing the legal implications of state laws that require alteration of the 'truthful
  > outputs of AI models.'"
  *** IMPORTANT: this statement is about AI models being made LESS accurate for ideological
  reasons — it is NOT about chatbots misleading customers. It cuts almost the opposite way.
  Do not use it as evidence the FTC is policing chatbot misrepresentation. ***

  One genuinely useful footnote from that Federal Register document (on disclaimers):
  > 51. ... see also FTC v. Corpay, Inc., 164 F.4th 807, 836 (11th Cir. Jan. 6, 2026)
  > (disclaimers or qualifying language may not correct a misleading impression when the
  > disclaimer "is small, ambiguous, or contradicted by the body of the ad")
  ^ This is a 2026 appellate holding on when disclaimers FAIL. Directly relevant to the
    Walmart-style disclaimer argument. NOT an AI case, but on point. Worth pulling.

- Bulk of 2025-26 US chatbot enforcement/litigation is (a) companion-chatbot child-safety
  (Character.AI, OpenAI — Garcia, Raine, Kentucky AG, Utah AG) and (b) CIPA/wiretap class
  actions over chat session recording. Neither is a misrepresentation-and-disclaimer story.
  - Baker Botts: https://ourtake.bakerbotts.com/post/102mipe/ai-chatbot-regulation-78-state-bills-58-lawsuits
    > "Baker Botts analysis of 284 deployer-facing AI litigation matters filed between January
    > 2020 and February 2026, drawn from CourtListener federal dockets, curated litigation
    > trackers, and FTC/State AG enforcement databases."
    > "Chatbot wiretap filings grew steadily from 2021 through 2023, then exploded in 2025 with
    > more new matters filed in a single year than in the prior four years combined. The claims
    > target deployers, not developers: it is the company that put the chatbot on its website,
    > not the vendor that built it, that faces the suit. Defendants in 2025 included healthcare
    > companies, insurance providers, dental practices, and universities."

## 4. Insurance / banking: NOT FOUND in the shape requested

No case located where a bank or insurer's chatbot misstated coverage, eligibility, a rate or a
fee and the customer was then held to or released from it. The insurance-and-AI literature in
2025-26 is about (a) insurers using AI to DENY claims (Cigna, UnitedHealth, Humana algorithmic
denial class actions) and (b) whether AI errors are insurable. Different stories.

One adjacent, genuinely interesting case (but inverted — the AI's user is the plaintiff's
opponent, and an insurer is suing the AI company):
- Nippon Life Insurance Company of America v. OpenAI Foundation and OpenAI Group PBC,
  No. 1:26-cv-02448 (N.D. Ill.), filed 4 March 2026.
  Claims: tortious interference with contract, abuse of process, unauthorized practice of law.
  Seeks $300,000 compensatory, $10 million punitive.
  - ABA Law Technology Today, 11 May 2026, by Alan Klevan:
    https://www.americanbar.org/groups/law_practice/resources/law-technology-today/2026/when-is-a-settlement-not-a-settlement-ai
    > "On March 4, 2026, Nippon Life sued OpenAI, alleging ChatGPT effectively practiced law by
    > helping a former claimant draft 44 post-settlement filings, including a fabricated case citation."
  - Epstein Becker Green: https://www.commerciallitigationupdate.com/the-case-was-settled-but-chatgpt-thought-otherwise-a-dispute-poised-to-define-ai-legal-liability
  This is NOT a "company disclaims its chatbot's promise" story. Noted for completeness only.

- Court allows discovery into an insurer's use of AI to deny claims (Hunton, 2026):
  https://www.hunton.com/hunton-insurance-recovery-blog/court-allows-discovery-into-insurers-use-of-ai-to-deny-claims
  > "if the denial is based on an AI hallucination that is not caught by a human, a policyholder
  > could use that to argue that the insurer acted in bad faith."
  Case name not captured. Would need chasing.

- **digitalapplied.com** — SEO content; makes specific unverified claims about FTC deadlines.
  Do not cite.
- **secondsidemedia.com** "Factual Clarification: Wolf River Electric" — a reputation-management
  style page. It happens to be factually careful, but it is a placed asset, not journalism.
  Do not cite; its underlying facts are available from Star Tribune / Volokh / the docket.
- **legalfinance.expert, blog.promise.legal, turleylaw.com** — plausible-looking legal content
  marketing. blog.promise.legal has the best articulation of the scope-of-authority disclaimer
  argument, but it is unsigned marketing copy. Use it for framing only, never as authority.
  Its useful line, for reference only:
  > "A statement like 'This AI assistant can provide pricing information and generate quotes up
  > to $5,000. It is not authorized to issue refunds, modify contract terms, or make binding
  > commitments on behalf of [Company]' does not eliminate apparent authority entirely, but it
  > provides evidentiary support that a third party's belief in broader authority was not reasonable."
