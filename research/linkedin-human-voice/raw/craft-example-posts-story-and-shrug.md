1h

- Report this post

Your LinkedIn post doesn’t need a tidy little lesson at the bottom. I know how that sounds. My whole industry is built on the neat takeaway. The “3 things this taught me.” The CTA that begs for a comment. The final line that turns a perfectly good observation into homework. I’ve taught that for years. I’ve changed my mind. A post still needs a point. It needs a reason to exist. It needs to make something sharper, clearer, funnier, more uncomfortable, or more true than it was before the reader got there. But it does not need to announce its own usefulness. People don’t remember the posts that politely handed them a lesson. They remember the ones that made something click. The thing they had been noticing but had not yet named. The tension they were already feeling. The sentence that made them think, “Yes. That.” If your thinking is good, the reader can tell. You don’t have to underline it for them. Someone interviewed me this week and asked whether a post still needs a clear takeaway. I said not really. You don’t need a line at the bottom going, “So, do you also think we should all eat more burgers?” That fell out of my mouth mid-sentence, and unfortunately it is now the sound every forced CTA makes to me. A manufactured takeaway isn’t service. It’s insecurity. It’s the writer not trusting the reader to keep up. What earns attention now is not the little lesson at the end. It’s having an original thought in the first place. Interesting beats instructive. So yes, you can retire “what do you think?” for good. This post doesn’t have a takeaway. You didn’t need one.

Random thoughts after a day of looking at industrial software companies:

Almost all of them use AI for the same thing.

Cleaning up data.
Making it searchable.
Nice dashboards.

"Does AI use too much energy?"

I think that whole conversation
is pointing in the wrong direction.

What problem disappears because of this?

If AI burns 1 kWh

the energy question answers itself.

But if it just makes data prettier,

Removing a real, expensive pain is.

Most of these companies are sitting on
decades of process knowledge and real customers.

They don't need more AI features.

They need to find the one problem
big enough to build a business on.

Anyway. Just thinking out loud.

# A small, unofficial translation guide for corporate announcements: | Iwan Pienaar · LinkedIn · 2026-03-23

**Iwan Pienaar**: High-Stakes Writer for Founders, Brands, & Strategic Agencies at Self-employed with 26 years 10 months of experience. Based in City of Johannesburg, Gauteng, South Africa [ZA].

---

A small, unofficial translation guide for corporate announcements:

“We are excited to announce…”
We are cautiously optimistic and hoping this lands well.

“This marks the next phase of our journey…”
We are not entirely sure how to describe this, but it sounds important.

“We remain committed to…”
Nothing has changed, but we would like credit for consistency.

“This reflects our continued focus on…”
We discussed this extensively and decided to keep it broad.

“We are uniquely positioned to…”
We would prefer not to compare ourselves too directly.

“This strategic initiative will…”
There is a plan. It is still evolving.

“We are pleased with the progress…”
It is going better than it was.

“This creates significant value for stakeholders…”
It should be good. In theory.

“This announcement underscores our vision…”
We needed a closing sentence.

None of these are technically wrong. They are just carefully constructed to survive approval rather than say something that might be challenged.

The irony is that the more polished the language becomes, the harder it is to tell what anyone is actually prepared to stand behind once it leaves the building.

Which is usually where the real work starts.

A Cute Conversation I Overheard in the Elevator
This morning, as I stepped into the elevator, I found myself riding with a grandfather and his granddaughter, who looked about three years old.
Noticing that we lived in the same apartment building, the grandfather gently encouraged her, saying, “You should say hello.”
With a bright smile, she looked up and said, “Hello.” to me.
I asked where she was going, and she excitedly replied, “To the playground!”
A moment later, I overheard her ask her grandfather:
“Grandpa, why do we swallow our saliva?”
My mind immediately started searching for an answer.
I've never really thought about it. If humans didn't swallow saliva, it would just dribble out. How do we do it automatically without even noticing? Children really are curious. I wonder how Grandpa is going to explain this.
Then came the grandfather's response:
“That's a good question. Why do you think we swallow it?”
And without missing a beat, the little girl replied:
“Because it's fun!”
I couldn't help but smile.
What struck me wasn't the question itself, but my own reaction. The moment I heard the question, I assumed she wanted an explanation. I was already preparing an answer in my head.
The grandfather, however, didn't rush to explain. Instead, he responded with curiosity.
Sometimes children ask questions because they are thinking out loud. Sometimes they are exploring ideas. Sometimes they are simply inviting us into their world.
As adults, we often feel responsible for having the right answer. Yet there may be moments when listening is more valuable than explaining, and curiosity more meaningful than expertise.
