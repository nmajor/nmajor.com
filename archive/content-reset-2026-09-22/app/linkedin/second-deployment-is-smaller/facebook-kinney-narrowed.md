Kinney Drugs, the pharmacy chain across New York and Vermont, put an AI assistant on its incoming patient calls in May. By August it was back to a touch-tone menu.

Patients had reported incoherent calls, wrong medication dosages, and missed prescription notifications.

The AI kept one job: outbound refill texts, to patients who asked to get them.

Look at the difference between those two jobs. On an incoming call, the thing has to understand a real person and get a dosage right. On an outbound text, the patient signed up for it and the worst case is a reminder they didn't need. Same software, same week. What changed is what it costs when it's wrong.

That turns out to be a pattern. Johnson & Johnson ran up nearly 9,000 AI projects before measuring which ones worked, then cut to four. Amazon pulled its cashierless checkout out of full-size supermarkets and kept it in small shops. Taco Bell slowed its drive-thru voice AI right down after an order for 18,000 cups of water went round the internet.

I wrote the whole thing up this week.
