In April a COO posted that he'd built an internal HR tool himself and avoided close to $100,000 in software. Seven weeks later he left the company.

Hans Kahler was COO at Brivo. The tool tracked HR objectives. He built it himself, and the number that travelled was close to $100,000 in software avoided.

A commenter wrote underneath that teams who build their own tools "own the long-term maintenance cost, which compounds over time."

Then he announced he was leaving Brivo after 13 years.

Nobody has said the tool broke. Nobody has said anything about it at all. The person who built it is gone, and there is no public record of who has it now.

That's not a scandal. It's the normal shape of these stories, which is that they stop.

I went looking for a mid-market company that had publicly documented abandoning an AI tool it built. I found none. Not one.

Every failure I could verify surfaced because something forced it out. A UK outsourcer's £239 million government pensions contract, priced on an assumption that 95% of transactions would be automated, came apart in front of a parliamentary committee that could compel the chief executive to testify. A government research office found under 3% of its AI-generated bill summaries met standard, and that came out in congressional testimony.

Committees, arbitrators, regulators. If you have none of those, you have no reason to publish. And nobody does.

Which is why the build-it-yourself record in your industry looks clean. Almost nobody in it has ever been made to open the books.
