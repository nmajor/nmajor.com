Okay, first of all, making it a token-maxing leaderboard scoreboard was silly and dumb. That's kind of like that Dilbert comic strip where they said, "Oh, we're going to start charging you for how many bugs you fix." I think part of it is a story of misaligned incentives.

I do think, personally, that AI can be a huge productivity boost for developers from my own experience, because I think it boosts my productivity a lot. It does shift the bottleneck, and I've even found myself running three or four parallel agents all working on different tasks. I found the bottleneck is that when they all finish, I have to check and do context switching for three or four or five different bugs, fixes, and features all at once. Now I've just created a huge, towering to-do list for myself, and that wasn't very efficient either.

Now I try to minimize the number of parallel agents down to two, just because I need to make sure they're approaching things the right way. I need to review their code before it's merged. Mostly, what I do is check if it looks and feels clean and if it feels like something I would write.

One productivity thing that just really boosted it is to have the AI check itself with Playwright and manually do end-to-end testing. That really helps a lot, and that's a huge productivity booster, especially when you're building a feature or fixing a bug that involves dozens of different permutations to test. That's a big lift.

I think, I don't know, maybe my take is a bit more nuanced. I think it's a universal truth, especially in engineering teams, that you get what you incentivize, and you can easily incentivize the wrong thing. At the end of the day, what should be incentivized is what should it be? I would have said two or three years ago, before all this AI stuff, that you have to incentivize high-quality code balanced with delivering on product features. Now, maybe it's the same. I don't know, maybe it's more about delivering on the product features, because at the end of the day, too, I feel like code is so cheap. A lot of our architecture patterns and stuff, like refactoring, are cheap too, so I think we haven't yet figured out how to utilize AI in its most productive form with software engineering. It might be the case that the most productive is to have it build prototypes, and then you pick the final result or something. It's maybe bake-offs, but also these things are the level of intelligence that you get per dollar, which does seem to be going down, so I think that'll balance out a lot.

My personal take: there are a lot of people saying, "Hey, AI is more expensive than the employees, blah blah blah." I don't think that will be the case in five years. I think it's the case only selectively now, not always. That said, AI still is super dumb at many, many things, including some types of software.
