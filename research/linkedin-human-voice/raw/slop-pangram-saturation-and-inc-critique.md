One in four longform posts on social media is fully AI-generated. Here on LinkedIn? Over 40%. Two months ago, we launched a Chrome extension that flags AI content in your feed, with an option for users to share anonymized detection data. We've now compiled those findings into a full report. LinkedIn was the most AI-saturated platform, with just over 40% of longform posts flagging as fully AI-generated. Likely exacerbated by built-in AI writing features, LinkedIn's slop problem is so bad that LinkedIn posts made up just under two-thirds of all AI content we flagged. Moderation is also an issue: LinkedIn clearly does not downrank AI content, despite claiming to. By contrast, Reddit's bot restrictions largely kept AI content out of replies. For top-level posts, that strategy wasn't as effective: 11.6% of original posts on Reddit were AI-generated. There's more in our full report, linked below. # LinkedIn Just Added a Button to Report AI Slop. There’s Just 1 Problem

Flagging AI slop is a good idea in principle, but skeptics have concerns. 

BY CHRIS MORRIS @MORRISATLARGE

Aug 3, 2026

Two months ago, LinkedIn vowed that it was planning to target low-quality AI posts that distract its users from finding value on the platform. Last week, it unveiled its most visible tool for doing so. But skeptics are already wondering if the feature will actually reduce AI slop or just make it harder for human eyes to detect.

On July 30, LinkedIn added a new feature that lets users click a “seems like AI slop” button when someone’s post appears to have been written with AI. The company says it will use that feedback to determine how much reach a post will get outside of the poster’s network.

“AI slop is a top priority for all of us,” wrote Hari Srinivasan, chief product officer at LinkedIn, in a post that hopefully wasn’t written using AI. “We really care about this. People come to LinkedIn to connect with real people and share their real perspectives, ideas and expertise.”

While the goal of the AI slop labeling button seems good, some parties remain suspicious, particularly because LinkedIn’s parent company, Microsoft, has a vested financial interest in the success of artificial intelligence.

An Inc.com Featured Presentation

“The platform that built an entire algorithm around AI-generated content now wants you to report it,” wrote Nick Bennett, founder of NB Marketing. “That’s like a casino adding a gambling addiction hotline to the slot machine screen.”

Other users weren’t happy that the “seems like AI slop” button will be used to train the company’s own AI classifying systems. (You can find the report button by clicking the three dots at the top of a user’s post, in the same pulldown menu as “save,” “copy link,” and other options.) And several noted that since this is a human-driven reporting feature, its results could be gamed or weaponized, making it less about spotting AI posts and more about silencing views that someone may disagree with

“The real challenge won’t be identifying AI-generated text, but maintaining trust that these signals reflect content quality rather than popularity, disagreement or coordinated reporting,” wrote Waldemar Ingdahl, senior communications officer at the Institute for Futures Studies, in a reply to Srinivasan. “That’s ultimately a governance problem as much as a technical one.”
