# What LinkedIn readers mean by "AI slop"

## Scope

This report turns the September 23 scan into writing rules for Nick's LinkedIn posts. The scan covered 96 items from Reddit, X, YouTube, TikTok, Instagram, and Hacker News. The source mix is useful for finding complaints and recurring tells. It cannot tell us how often any one pattern causes a report.

LinkedIn's feedback button also creates a basic ambiguity: a report means that a member thought a post looked like AI slop. It does not prove that AI wrote the post.

## The complaint is mostly about substance

LinkedIn's own description, captured in the [raw research](raw/linkedin-ai-slop-writing-tells-and-reader-complaints-raw.md), centers on low-effort material that sounds polished but has no clear point of view, unique perspective, or substance. Its feed guidance also names repetitive posts and engagement bait. That is a better working definition than any punctuation test.

The reaction is now consequential. LinkedIn said more than one million members had used the feedback control, and coverage reported about 40% fewer views for content caught by its systems and member feedback. The exact split between automated detection and member reports is not public. [Yahoo coverage](https://finance.yahoo.com/technology/ai/articles/linkedin-seems-ai-slop-button-195000046.html) and an [Instagram summary of LinkedIn's announcement](https://www.instagram.com/reel/DcbYwXNMhwC/) both carry those figures. Two X posts repeat them, but those are commentary, not independent confirmation: [Oliver Clingain](https://x.com/Oliver_Clingain/status/2101849140590481815) and [Logan Gott](https://x.com/LoganTGott/status/2099862277004419236).

Readers seem to object to posts that make them spend time without giving them anything back. The common failures are:

- no claim a reasonable person could disagree with
- no firsthand detail, named source, number, or constraint
- a familiar idea stretched into many short lines
- a generic moral added after the evidence
- an image that looks polished but says nothing specific about the story
- a prompt for comments standing in for a conclusion

A post can avoid every stereotyped AI word and still fail this test. Clean prose with no judgment is still empty.

## Surface tells still matter

The scan repeatedly surfaced em dashes, emoji bullets, forced groups of three, "not just X, but Y," unusually smooth transitions, and conclusions that tie every thread into a neat bow. AI-generated images can make readers suspicious before they read the first sentence. The current backlash is visible in videos such as [BusinessCringe's commentary](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=B4RIndErtZc) and [Sarah In Practice's discussion of AI comments](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KR-yiRwQu98).

These are suspicion markers, not proof. The academic paper summarized in the raw file found that linguistic features separating AI and human text did not predict which human comments people accused of being AI. The accusation has become a form of gatekeeping. Human writers use em dashes. AI can write without them.

Our process should remove the obvious packaging because Nick already dislikes it. We should not chase an imaginary detector score or make the prose worse on purpose. The target is a post with enough source-specific detail and judgment that the authorship question stops being interesting.

## Rules to add to the LinkedIn process

Run this audit after the ordinary voice and source checks.

1. **Write the claim in one sentence.** If the claim is only "AI is changing business," the post is not ready.
2. **Put a receipt near the top.** Name the company, source, measured result, date, or strange implementation detail in the first two paragraphs.
3. **Make one judgment.** Nick should say what he thinks about the evidence. Neutral summaries read like generated research notes.
4. **Keep the uncertainty attached to the claim.** Say what the study measured, what the company did not disclose, or why two figures are not comparable. This adds substance and prevents inflated claims.
5. **Delete the moral.** No "the lesson is," "you need to," diagnostic question, or advice tacked onto the end. Stop on the evidence or a direct observation about it.
6. **Break the template.** Reject posts with repeated one-line paragraphs, symmetrical contrast pairs, a forced list of three, or a tidy recap. Paragraph shape should follow the material.
7. **Read for stock packaging.** Remove em dashes, emoji structure, "not just X," evaluative "the X part" preambles, suspense openers, and polished transition words.
8. **Demand a source-specific image.** A real marked-up source, a classic meme tied to the claim, or a deliberately imperfect workbench scene can work. A generic glowing robot cannot.
9. **Do not manufacture humanity.** No fake typo, fake confession, invented anecdote, or staged outrage. Irregular rhythm is useful. Fake mess is another formula.
10. **Use the accusation test last.** Ask, "If someone called this AI slop, what concrete value in the post would make that claim look lazy?" If there is no good answer, add evidence or kill the post.

No detector should approve copy. A detector can spot repeated phrases, but it cannot decide whether Nick has made a useful point.

## The four Farmers follow-up posts

### Value dashboard

This has the strongest angle. "Google counts silence as success" is specific, surprising, and easy to test against the documentation. The current draft waits until the last paragraph to make its judgment, and the paired "Maybe... Maybe..." sentences look composed.

Open with the counting rule. Keep the three-minute assumption and wage calculation. Then say plainly that the dashboard estimates saved time instead of observing it. A marked-up screenshot of Google's success definition is the right image because the source itself is the hook.

### Support-agent study

The evidence is good, but the draft reads like a careful abstract. The last paragraph uses the familiar generated shape of "this is strong evidence" followed by "it also found."

Tie the study back to the Farmers claim and make the comparison Nick's judgment. A measured 15% lift across 5,172 agents tells us more than a large aggregate hours figure with no disclosed attribution. Keep the experience-level split and the caveat about manager requests. Use a workbench image only if it shows the actual figures and source. The paper's table or abstract would be safer.

### RAG refusal

The opening number earns attention, and the draft correctly says the benchmark did not test Farmers. The middle still sounds like a paper summary. Terms such as "conflict-recognition result" need either a plain explanation or removal.

Connect the benchmark to the missing disclosure without implying that Farmers performed badly. Farmers reports resolution. It does not report correctness, refusals, or escalation. End there. A source screenshot with the 42.7% refusal result marked is more credible than an abstract AI illustration.

### Search bot in 2003

This is the most naturally human post because the date is funny and the comparison punctures current hype. It has a named company, branch count, before-and-after relevance figures, and the operational work behind the result.

The final two sentences are too neat. Make the judgment more direct: the chat interface changed; the work of teaching search the company's language did not change much. A scan or screenshot that looks like a 2003 trade-publication clipping will carry the contrast better than a generated office scene. Do not fake a clipping. Use the real article capture or label a reconstruction clearly.

## Recommended gate

A post passes when it has one checkable claim, one source-specific detail near the top, one honest judgment, and no generic moral. It fails when polish is doing the work that evidence or opinion should do.
