## Draft A

# The AI came second. The research came first.

Summary: Farmers says its Agency Servicing Efficiency 35 program cut routine agent work by 35%, about 16.4 million hours a year, and the reported reason it worked is the order of operations: field research first, AI tools second.

Farmers says its agents and their staff now spend 16.4 million fewer hours a year on routine servicing work. The figure comes from Agency Servicing Efficiency 35, a two-year program covering more than 8,000 agents and roughly 20,000 staff, and the company reports it hit the 35% reduction target it set in June 2024.

Both numbers are company-reported. Phil Leininger, a Farmers executive, gave the details in an [interview Insurance Business published on August 26](https://www.insurancebusinessmag.com/us/news/technology/how-farmers-used-ai-to-free-up-16-4-million-agent-hours-587592.aspx), and Farmers repeated the headline figures on [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/philipleininger_how-farmers-used-ai-to-free-up-164-million-activity-7498423667673141248-FYb3). No independent audit exists. I think it's worth taking seriously anyway, because how the work got done is the useful part.

Leininger says Farmers started with the people doing the work. The company asked agents and their staff which daily tasks added no value, using office visits, forums, listening sessions, and direct conversations. Then it picked a high-volume place to start: personal auto, home, and umbrella insurance, where most of the policy volume sat.

The clearest AI change came after that groundwork. askfarmers.ai replaced five separate search systems with one conversational interface, covering more than 300,000 internal documents, notifications, and articles on breaking news, policies, procedures, rates, and forms, according to [Leininger's interview with Insurance Business](https://www.insurancebusinessmag.com/us/news/technology/how-farmers-used-ai-to-free-up-164-million-agent-hours-587592.aspx). He says it now resolves more than 60% of the questions that used to become calls to a service center.

He describes the method as "make it work, make it fast, make it easy." Reliability first, then speed, then simplicity. The program reached across the company too, pulling in underwriting, product, distribution, agency partners, back-office teams, and sales. Leininger calls it an enterprise initiative rather than a service-center project.

Leininger also reports [54% fewer inbound calls and chats for new business, 23% fewer for existing business](https://www.insurancebusinessmag.com/us/news/technology/how-farmers-used-ai-to-free-up-164-million-agent-hours-587592.aspx), and a service team kept at the same size while the portfolio grew. None of those numbers comes with volumes, dates, or comparison periods, so they're hard to weigh from the outside. In August, he said the program had moved into phase two, taking the same approach to specialty and business insurance.

The arithmetic needs some rounding to reconcile. Leininger says Farmers calculated that agents and staff spent close to 50 million hours a year servicing existing customers before the program. Thirty-five percent of exactly 50 million is 17.5 million, so a 16.4 million-hour cut implies a baseline nearer 47 million, which squares with the "close to" phrasing. Spread across the 28,000 agents and staff in the interview, it works out to about 586 hours per person a year, or 11 hours a week. A part-time job's worth of not doing paperwork. That's a scale check, not a claim the savings landed evenly.

"Freed" isn't cash, either. Leininger frames the hours as economic value returned to agents, based on the cost of customer-service staff, but no wage assumption or dollar figure has been published. The 35% also bundles process simplification with the AI tools, so the reporting can't say what the tool alone did.

Farmers didn't start with a model and go hunting for somewhere to put it. It asked the people doing the work which tasks ate their time, studied the answers, simplified the work, and built the tool around problems it had already found. That's the depth of research it takes to identify where AI can actually make a difference. The AI came second. The research came first.

## Draft B

Title: Farmers asked first

Summary: Farmers Insurance says its Agency Servicing Efficiency 35 program cut routine servicing work for 28,000 agents and staff by 35%, about 16.4 million hours a year, and it got there by asking agents which tasks wasted their time before it built anything.

Farmers Insurance says it has cut the routine servicing work of its more than 8,000 agents and their roughly 20,000 staff by 35%, about 16.4 million hours a year. The claim comes from Phil Leininger, the Farmers executive who described the program, in an [Insurance Business interview](https://www.insurancebusinessmag.com/us/news/technology/how-farmers-used-ai-to-free-up-164-million-agent-hours-587592.aspx) published August 26. Farmers [repeated both figures on LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/philipleininger_how-farmers-used-ai-to-free-up-164-million-activity-7498423667673141248-FYb3) the same day, and Leininger reshared the post. No outside party has audited any of it.

The program is called Agency Servicing Efficiency 35. Farmers launched it in June 2024 with a two-year target, after calculating that agents and staff spent close to 50 million hours a year servicing existing customers. Leininger says the program reached its 35% target this year and has moved into a second phase covering specialty and business insurance.

Farmers started by asking.

Before building anything, it sent people into agent offices, ran forums and listening sessions, and put a plain question to the people serving customers: which recurring tasks eat your time without adding value? It began in personal lines, auto, home, and umbrella, because that is where most of the policy volume sits. And it pulled underwriting, product, distribution, agency partners, and back-office teams into the work rather than leaving it to a service center.

Then it simplified the processes those conversations surfaced. Only after that did it build tools.

The one tool described in detail is askfarmers.ai. Agents used to search five separate systems for breaking news, policies, procedures, rates, and forms. Farmers consolidated more than 300,000 documents, notifications, and articles into one conversational interface, and Leininger says it now resolves more than 60% of the questions that used to become calls to a service center.

The other reported numbers point the same way. Inbound phone and chat contacts from agents fell 54% for new business and 23% for existing business. The service team stayed the same size while the book grew. The build sequence, Leininger says, was "make it work, make it fast, make it easy": reliability first, then speed, then a simpler experience.

Now the caveats, because they are real. Every operating figure traces back to one interested source. The interview gives no measurement method, no call volumes, no time-study detail, and no definition of a resolved question. The 50 million-hour baseline is an executive assertion. The 16.4 million hours are an estimate of freed time rather than realized savings. Leininger values the hours at the average cost of customer-service or office staff, but Farmers discloses no wage assumption and no dollar figure.

The arithmetic at least hangs together. A 35% cut yielding 16.4 million hours implies a baseline near 47 million, which fits the looser "close to 50 million." Spread evenly across those 28,000 people, and there is no reason to think it was even, the estimate comes to roughly 11 hours per person per week. Large, but not absurd for people whose days were partly spent hunting through five systems for the same form.

I find the size of the claim plausible, and the reason is the sequence. Farmers did not buy a generic AI capability and go looking for somewhere to put it. It found specific friction first, in the words of the people doing the work, and the clearest AI change removed a concrete piece of it: five search systems became one place to ask a question. The evidence cannot say how much of the 35% came from the tool and how much came from the process simplification around it. Farmers' own wording credits both.

What the account does show, as well as any case reported this year, is the depth of research required to identify where AI can actually make a difference.

## Draft C

**Farmers didn't start with the AI.**

Farmers says a two-year program cut routine agent servicing work by 35%, freeing 16.4 million hours a year, and the AI was the last thing built.

Farmers Insurance says its agents and their staff used to spend close to 50 million hours a year servicing existing customers. After a two-year program called Agency Servicing Efficiency 35, they say that figure is down by 16.4 million hours, or about 35%.

All of those numbers come from Farmers, mostly through an interview the company's head of AI and emerging technology, Phil Leininger, gave to Insurance Business in August ([Insurance Business, August 26, 2026](https://www.insurancebusinessmag.com/us/news/technology/how-farmers-used-ai-to-free-up-164-million-agent-hours-587592.aspx)). Neither the methodology nor the underlying data appears in the public reporting. Treat this as company-reported, not audited.

Leininger says Farmers did not begin with a model and look for a place to put it. The first move was to ask the agents and their roughly 20,000 support staff which recurring tasks added no value. Office visits, forums, listening sessions, direct conversations. The starting point was personal auto, home, and umbrella, because that is where the volume sits.

Then came process simplification. Leininger calls the sequence "make it work, make it fast, make it easy." Then came the AI: askfarmers.ai, a conversational interface that pulls from more than 300,000 internal documents, notifications, and articles that used to live across five separate systems covering breaking news, policies, procedures, rates, and forms.

Leininger says askfarmers.ai now resolves more than 60% of questions that previously became calls to a service center, and that inbound phone and chat interactions dropped 54% for new business and 23% for existing business. He also says the service team held steady while the portfolio grew. The sources give no absolute volumes, no comparison period, no definition of "resolves," and no headcount ([Insurance Business](https://www.insurancebusinessmag.com/us/news/technology/how-farmers-used-ai-to-free-up-164-million-agent-hours-587592.aspx)).

The headline figures can be checked against each other, sort of. Thirty-five percent of exactly 50 million is 17.5 million. A 16.4 million reduction at 35% implies a baseline of about 46.9 million, which fits the loose phrase "close to 50 million." Divide 16.4 million hours across roughly 28,000 agents and staff and you get around 11 hours a week per person. None of this proves the work was evenly distributed, or that "freed" hours became selling time. It just sets the scale of the claim.

What makes the story plausible, on the evidence available, is the order and scope. Farmers did not start with a generic AI capability and search for somewhere to place it. It collected specific complaints from the people doing the work, picked the highest-volume line of business, and pulled underwriting, product, distribution, agency partners, back-office teams, and sales into the same room. The clearest AI change then replaced a concrete source of friction: five search systems and hundreds of thousands of scattered items collapsed into one place to ask a question.

Farmers is now in what Leininger calls phase two, applying the work to specialty and business insurance ([Insurance Business](https://www.insurancebusinessmag.com/us/news/technology/how-farmers-used-ai-to-free-up-164-million-agent-hours-587592.aspx)). Whether the same numbers show up in lines that aren't personal auto and home, the reporting does not say.

This is one company telling one trade publication about its own results, in its own words, on its own terms. The strongest read is not that 16.4 million hours is a fact. The strongest read is that a company with eight thousand agents and twenty thousand support staff spent real time, before any AI, figuring out which work was actually worth doing.

The AI was the last thing they built.
