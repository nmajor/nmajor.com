I've used two AI case studies in my own newsletter as evidence that building your own was working. Neither has a number in it dated after year one.

A public school district in Washington got a lot of attention, including from me. I linked it in June as up to $250,000 in software contracts saved by staff who don't write code.

The number moves depending on where you read it. K12 Dive reported $250k in April. An Education Week piece in May had $220k. By late May the district's own Kris Hagel was back at $250k. An April interview has no dollar figure at all, plus a detail that undercuts the arithmetic: the Claude Code spend is about $100 a month, where earlier tellings said $200.

Hagel's own follow-up lands somewhere honest. "The dollars were never the point," he wrote. "The precedent was."

I'd agree, and I'd add the thing nobody put next to it. Over roughly the same stretch, that board approved a $4.8 million four-year Apple lease.

The saving everyone wrote about is around 4.6% of one hardware contract.

Then FranklinCovey, which I've cited too. The figure that travelled was about $50,000 a year on a finance app. An earlier Make.com customer story from six months before says $60,000, in a different department, with a headline claim of HR workflows going "from 30 days to 2 hours" where the later piece describes a fiscal binder going from 30 days to a few days.

Same company, same story, different numbers and different speed.

None of this means the projects failed. Both may be fine. But the number you're about to put in a board deck has already changed twice on its way to you, and nobody has published what any of it cost in year two.

I'm not exempt from that. I passed both of these along.
