Feed

Engineering the next generation of LinkedIn’s Feed

Authored byHristo Danchev

March 12, 2026

The LinkedIn Feed serves more than 1.3 billion professionals, each on a unique career journey. Whether members are building their brand, sharing expertise, exploring new ideas, or learning from trusted voices, they come to LinkedIn to connect and grow. We want to connect every member to insights, ideas, and inspiration that move them forward. The most valuable content is timely, relevant to their professional goals, and grounded in trust.

While the Feed has long been AI-powered, recent LLM advances gave us the opportunity to rethink what's possible. That’s why we’re rolling out a new advanced ranking system, powered by LLMs and GPUs, that better understands what a post is actually about and how it relates to a member's evolving interests and career goals. By learning from past LinkedIn activities, it delivers more accurate and helpful recommendations than ever before. 

We’re thrilled to share how we built our new system with state-of-the-art LLMs and transformer AI models to create a more engaging, personalized, and timely Feed experience that surfaces genuinely valuable content, whether it comes from your immediate network or professionals you’ve never connected with before.

In this blog, we’ll explore the nuances of engineering and scaling our next-generation Feed.

The challenge: serving over one billion professionals at scale

Every time you open LinkedIn and scroll through your Feed, you’re experiencing one of the largest-scale recommendation systems in the industry. Our challenge at hand is to deliver personalized content at speed, balancing content from your network, who you follow, and the LinkedIn Economic Graph more broadly (i.e. suggested content).

There is a complex decision behind every post you see. Our systems evaluate a number of signals, like information you’ve chosen to share on your own profile such as industry, experience, skills and geography. Then, the system looks at how you engage with content over time, including what you’ve read, liked, commented on, returned back to, or simply scrolled past. These historical interactions, namely what do you engage with or repeatedly skip, and how are these engagement patterns changing over time, combined with the aforementioned profile signals are parsed by the models to deliver a personalized Feed just for YOU – all within a blink of an eye. Under the hood we rank millions of posts at any given time, while balancing freshness with relevance across a constantly evolving content bank.

Our traditional approach used multiple retrieval sources - trending content, collaborative filtering, and embedding-based systems, each maintaining separate infrastructure. This created engineering complexity and made holistic optimization difficult. Meanwhile, our ranking model treated each impression independently, missing the sequential patterns in how professionals actually consume content over time.

After some experimentation we converged onto a hybrid complementary relevance solution - a unified retrieval system leveraging advances in LLMs to generate a high-quality representation of our members and content, and a sequential ranking model that captures how professionals engage with content over time, all while efficiently utilizing our GPU fleet.

Unified retrieval through fine-tuned LLMs

For years, the LinkedIn Feed retrieval relied on a heterogeneous architecture. When you opened your Feed, content came from multiple specialized sources: a chronological index of your network's activities, trending posts in your geography, collaborative filtering based on similar members' interests, industry-specific trending content, and several embedding-based retrieval systems. Each source had its own infrastructure, index structure, and optimization strategy. This architecture worked and it enabled us to surface diverse, relevant content, but it came with substantial maintenance costs of separate systems for trending content, collaborative filtering, historical feeds, and topic-based retrieval. 

So we asked ourselves: what if we could replace this multi-source complexity with a single, unified retrieval system powered by LLM-generated embeddings? And what if those embeddings could capture an understanding so rich that they understood not just keyword matches, but the deeper professional interests that make content relevant?

In practical terms, if you happen to be interested in "electrical engineering" but engage heavily with posts about “small modular reactors,” traditional keyword-based systems might miss the connection. Our LLM-based retrieval understands these topics are semantically related because the underlying language model brings world knowledge learned from its massive pretraining corpus - it knows that electrical engineers often work on power grid optimization, renewable energy integration, and the infrastructure challenges. This knowledge proves especially powerful for cold-start scenarios: this is when a new member joins LinkedIn with only a profile headline and job title, the LLM can deduce likely interests and relevant content areas based on member-provided information without waiting for engagement history to accumulate. For contrast, our non-LLM embedding could understand shallow semantic correlation like power, energy, electronics, etc., but not as deep as the renewable energy one, which only comes from world knowledge from the LLM.

Central to this effort is our commitment to responsible AI. We regularly and rigorously audit our models to check two things: that posts from different creators compete on equal footing, and that the scrolling experience, i.e. what you see in the feed, is consistent across audiences. 

Key engineering insight: LLM embeddings generalize beyond observed behavior. Assessing latent interests from world knowledge where collaborative filtering and keyword matching fall short, especially critical for cold-start users and sparse content niches.

For you, this means discovering content from authors you don't follow, on topics you care about, expressed in different terminology than you might use - dramatically expanding your access to relevant professional insights.

From structured data to effective prompts

The first hurdle of large scale retrieval: converting structured data into text that LLMs can process effectively. We built a "prompt library" that transforms features into templated sequences. For posts, we include format, author information (name, headline, company, industry), engagement counts, article metadata, and post text. For members, we incorporate profile information, skills, work history, education, and crucially - a chronologically ordered sequence of posts they've previously engaged with, which we refer to as “member engagement history.”

One of our most important discoveries came from experimenting with numerical features. Initially, we passed raw engagement counts as raw unprocessed features, so the model treated them just like any other text tokens. For example: a post with 12,345 views would simply appear as “views:12345” in the prompt. The model treated these digits just like any other text token, resulting in sub-par performance, as we observed near zero correlation (-0.004) between item popularity counts and the cosine similarly scores between member and item embeddings. This was alarming because item popularity is such a strong relevance signal: posts that many people find valuable are more likely to be found as such to any given member.

To overcome this problem, we took a different approach by breaking these counts into percentile buckets and then wrapped them with special tokens so that the model can distinguish them from unstructured text tokens. This turned “views:12345” into <view_percentile>71</view_percentile>,” indicating that this post is in the 71st percentile of view counts. The model could then learn stable representations for low popularity (0-20%), average (40-60%), high (90%+).

With values between 1-100, most percentiles tokenize as a single token or at most two tokens. This aligns with how tokenizer works by representing counts as single tokens, the model encodes them meaningfully in embeddings. A percentile of "71" becomes a single token, making it easier for the model to learn that this token indicates "above average” popularity. The correlation between popularity features and embedding similarity jumped 30x, while perhaps more importantly - recall@10 improved by 15%, suggesting that better feature encoding translates directly to better retrieval quality. Naturally, we applied the same strategy to other numerical features, such as engagement rates, recency signals, affinity scores, etc. 

Key engineering insight: LLMs don’t inherently understand magnitude, so raw numerical features tokenize poorly and lose ordinal meaning. Converting continuous values to percentile buckets wrapped in special tokens gives the model a stable, learnable vocabulary for quantity.

Training dual encoders at scale

We distilled and fine-tuned a LLM using millions of member-to-item pairs from Feed engagement data. The dual encoder architecture uses a LLM to process both the member and item prompts, and in return it generates embeddings that are compared via cosine similarity. We optimized for retrieval using InfoNCE loss with sophisticated negative sampling. Each positive member-item pair is contrasted against two types of negatives: easy and hard.

Easy negatives are randomly sampled posts that weren't shown to this member; these provide weak but stable contrastive signals. Hard negatives are posts that were actually impressed to the member but received no engagement, which turned out to be challenging cases where the model must learn nuanced distinctions between "relevant but not quite right" and "genuinely valuable." Adding just two hard negatives per member improved recall by +3.6%, which is a significant gain from a simple change.

We also discovered that carefully curating what goes into the member's interaction history is crucial. Initially, we included all impressed posts — both those members engaged with and those they scrolled past. This turned out to not only hurt model performance, but also was a lot more expensive in terms of compute capacity, since GPU compute is quadratically proportional to context length. When we filtered to include only posts that received positive engagement, the model learned much more effectively. 

In our production configuration with 8 H100 GPUs for training, the positives-only approach enabled:

  • 37% reduction in per-sequence memory footprint at equivalent history depth
  • Ability to process 40% more training sequences per batch given fixed GPU memory
  • 2.6× faster training iteration due to reduced sequence length and improved batching efficiency

These efficiency gains compound: faster training enables more experimentation, more experimentation enables better hyperparameter tuning, and better tuning enables better final models. The positives-only insight thus contributed both directly (better signal quality) and indirectly (more efficient experimentation) to our final model quality. Furthermore, the positives-only approach with the same context budget uses significantly less GPU memory, while achieving better recall.

Key engineering insight: The model learns better from understanding what you chose to engage with than from the full set of what you were shown. Hard negatives matter more, while a curated training signal beats scaling it. When a single change improves signal quality and reduces compute cost, the gains compound multiplicatively, so consider prioritizing those changes first.

What this means for your Feed experience: If you've spent the week engaging with posts about hospital operations, then like an article about cybersecurity risk, the system evaluates both your interest in operational responsibilities and your concern about digital infrastructure. The next post you see might be about “protecting patient data at scale,” connecting both threads in a way that keyword matching never could.

Online serving: freshness at scale

The embedding approach enables a powerful production architecture. We maintain three nearline pipelines running continuously:

  1. Prompt generation captures what’s happening on LinkedIn as it unfolds in near-realtime such as item creations, updates, and member activities. When a post is published or gains engagement, we update its item prompt to reflect the new state. When you update your profile or engage with content, we update your member prompt to reflect your evolving interests. These prompts are pushed to key-value stores for online access during retrieval, and simultaneously sent to a nearline stream processor for generating embeddings. The entire process happens within minutes, ensuring that what you see reflects the latest activity across the platform.
  2. Embedding generation feeds these fresh prompts through LLM inference servers running on clusters of GPUs. We batch updates in configurable time windows, trading off GPU efficiency against embedding freshness. For new posts, we generate embeddings in near-real time. For existing posts gaining engagement, we dynamically refresh embeddings, ensuring popularity signals stay current.
  3. GPU-accelerated indexing ingests item embeddings into a specialized index optimized for exhaustive nearest neighbor search on GPUs. When you open your Feed, we fetch your member embedding and run k-nearest-neighbor search against this index, retrieving the top candidates in sub-50ms despite the index containing millions of posts. This architecture achieves what initially seemed contradictory: sub-second retrieval latency serving thousands of queries per second while maintaining embeddings fresh enough to capture trending content and your evolving interests throughout the day.

Key engineering insight: Freshness and latency are not inherently at odds. Decoupling the system into nearline pipelines (prompt generation, embedding inference, and index updates) lets each stage optimize independently for its own latency-throughput tradeoff while keeping the end-to-end system fresh within minutes.

What does this look like in practice: When industry news breaks and relevant posts start getting traction, you see them within minutes, not hours. When you engage with content signaling a new professional interest, subsequent Feed visits reflect that updated understanding almost immediately. The system feels responsive because it is continuously updating its understanding of both content and member interests.

Consolidating complexity

The impact of this unified retrieval system extends beyond quality metrics. By replacing multiple separate retrieval sources with a single embedding-based approach, we cut down engineering complexity. Teams can now iterate on retrieval quality by improving the embeddings through better prompts, training objectives, or model architectures, rather than optimizing heterogeneous systems independently.

This simplification also benefits the ranking layer. Instead of receiving candidates from disparate sources with different biases and quality profiles, ranking receives a coherent set of candidates all selected through semantic similarity. This makes ranking's job easier and its optimizations more effective.

Ranking: understanding your professional journey

While retrieval determines which posts reach the ranking stage, ranking determines what a member actually sees in their Feed. Traditional ranking models evaluate each impression independently: given a member and a post, predict the likelihood of various engagement actions. This approach works but misses something fundamental: how you engage with content isn't random, rather it follows patterns that emerge from your professional journey.

So we built a Generative Recommender (GR) model for LinkedIn’s Feed that reimagines ranking by treating your feed interaction history as a sequence - a professional story told through the posts you've engaged with over time. Instead of scoring each post in isolation, GR processes more than a thousand of your historical interactions to understand temporal patterns and long-term interests. As with retrieval, the ranking model relies on professional signals and engagement patterns, never demographic attributes, and is regularly audited for equitable treatment across our member base.

The real-world difference in your Feed: When you engage with a post about machine learning on Monday, then interact with distributed systems content on Tuesday, and open LinkedIn again on Wednesday, our model understands these aren't random events. It's the natural continuation of a learning journey. Traditional models see three independent decisions; a sequential model understands the trajectory.

For newer members, GR sequential learning means the system gets smarter with every interaction. Your first few engagements on LinkedIn immediately start shaping your Feed, helping you discover relevant content and communities faster than conventional approaches that require extensive interaction history to make good predictions.

Key engineering insight: Treating interactions as an ordered sequence rather than independent events lets the model capture trajectory, not just preference. This is especially high-leverage for sparse users: a sequential model extracts more signal from fewer interactions than a pointwise model ever can.

Teaching transformers to recommend

The GR model uses industry leading transformer architecture with causal attention, processing your posts chronologically alongside your actions on each post. We capture rich features such as viewer information (profile, headline, company, industry), content embeddings, engagement signals, and post metadata, while your actions (long dwells, likes, comments, shares) are embedded and interleaved with post representations, creating a unified sequence that captures both what you saw and how you engaged.

These interleaved post-action pairs flow through multiple transformer layers with causal attention, meaning each position can only attend to previous positions-mimicking the actual temporal flow of how you experienced content. The transformer's self-attention mechanism allows the model to weigh different parts of your history based on relevance: recent posts might matter more for predicting immediate interests, but posts from two weeks ago about a specific technology might suddenly become relevant if recent activity suggests renewed interest in that area.

After the transformer layer, we employ a technique called late fusion, where we concatenate the transformer output with per-timestep context features such as device type, member profile embeddings, and aggregated count/affinity features. This fused representation is then processed by a Multi-gate Mixture-of-Experts (MMoE) prediction head, with shared DCNv2 experts gated per task. Because the transformer has already captured feature interactions through self-attention, the head's bottleneck shifts to task-specific routing: passive tasks (click, skip, long-dwell) and active tasks (like, comment, share) get specialized gating, while sharing the same powerful sequential representations.

Although sequential recommenders may work well without count features in some scenarios, we found them to be still very effective for our use case. Not only did they allow us to keep the sequence length under control, they were also very effective at capturing the viewer’s affinity to other actors. Including these features in the sequence pathway would inflate computational cost without clear benefit, so by fusing them after sequence processing, we get the best of both worlds: rich sequential understanding plus important contextual signals that drive relevance.

Key engineering insight: Not every feature benefits from full self-attention. Late fusion, injecting count and affinity signals after the transformer, avoids quadratic cost inflation on features whose value comes from independent signal strength, not sequential interaction. Place features where they pay for themselves computationally.

What you’ll notice when scrolling: The posts you see aren't just "relevant to your profile," rather they reflect where your professional interests are heading based on recent activity. If you've been exploring a new area, the Feed leans into that exploration, surfacing progressively deeper content. If your interests shift, the model adapts within the same session, making the Feed feel dynamic and responsive to your current mindset.

Engineering for production scale

The most sophisticated models are useless if they can't serve real traffic. Our third major innovation addresses a fundamental challenge: how do you deploy transformer models requiring massive GPU resources at LinkedIn's scale while maintaining millisecond latencies?

The GPU economics challenge

Our historical ranking models ran on CPUs, leveraging decades of optimization for dense layer computations. Transformers are fundamentally different. The self-attention scales quadratically with sequence length, and the massive parameter counts (billions) require high-bandwidth memory only available on GPUs. But GPUs are expensive, and cost-per-inference becomes critical at our scale.

For you as a member, this isn't just an infrastructure detail - it determines whether we can afford to give everyone the benefit of sophisticated AI models, or only serve them to high-engagement users. Our goal: make state of the art models economically viable at scale, ensuring every professional gets the best possible experience.

Training

Training sequential models on millions of member histories, each containing hundreds or thousands of interactions, generates enormous computational demands. We invested heavily in training infrastructure to make this practical.

  • A custom C++ data loader eliminates Python's multiprocessing overhead by fusing padding, batching, and packing operations at the native code level, reducing training step time substantially. Custom CUDA kernels for multi-label AUC computation drop metric calculation time from being a significant bottleneck to negligible overhead. Fused optimizer implementations reduce gradient descent step time by eliminating redundant GPU synchronizations.
  • For evaluation, we parallelized across all checkpoints simultaneously rather than serializing evaluation runs. This fully utilizes GPU memory and substantially reduces overall pipeline time, enabling faster iteration on model architectures and training strategies.

These optimizations collectively make the difference between "interesting research idea" and "production-grade system." Without them, training times would be prohibitively expensive and iteration cycles would be too slow for rapid experimentation.

Serving

The sequential architecture presents serving challenges. Processing 1,000+ historical interactions through multiple transformer layers requires substantial computation-far more than traditional point-wise ranking models. Meeting our sub-second latency requirement demanded custom infrastructure.

  • Disaggregated architecture that separates CPU-bound feature processing from GPU-heavy model inference.
  • Shared context batching approach computing the history representation once, then scoring all candidates in parallel using custom attention masks. Custom attention kernels logic optimized for our specific use case. 
  • PyTorch-native inference serving stack with I/O and compute optimizations achieved through zero-copy data movement, columnar layouts, and SIMD vectorization, enabling thousands of operations per second and significantly boosting production throughput.
  • Optimized attention computation with GRMIS (Generative Recommender Multi-Item Scoring), a custom Flash Attention variant that natively supports our attention pattern, delivering an additional 2× speedup over PyTorch's standard scaled dot-product attention implementation.

Key engineering insight: The bottleneck is rarely the model architecture, it's everything around it. On the training side, consider optimizing the scaffolding before scaling the compute. On the serving side, look for problem-specific invariants before reaching for generic compression. Invest in infrastructure that accelerates iteration; production efficiency comes from specialization.

All of these achievements serve a single purpose: delivering a smarter, faster, more responsive Feed without you ever noticing the complexity underneath. The models are more sophisticated than ever, yet your experience remains seamless-content loads instantly, recommendations feel more relevant, and trending conversations reach you while they still matter.

Conclusion

When you open LinkedIn tomorrow and find that perfect post about a topic you care about - perhaps from someone outside your network, but whose expertise is exactly what you need - that's the result of transformer models processing thousands of interactions, embeddings learned from millions of examples, and systems optimized to serve predictions in milliseconds.

The Feed you see today is smarter, more personal, and more relevant than ever. These systems represent more than incremental improvements, moreso they demonstrate significant evolution in how we approach building recommendation systems at scale, combining the semantic understanding of large language models with production-grade engineering to deliver better experiences for professionals worldwide.

We're excited about where this technology can take us next, and we're committed to continuing to innovate on behalf of the 1.3 billion professionals who rely on LinkedIn every day.
