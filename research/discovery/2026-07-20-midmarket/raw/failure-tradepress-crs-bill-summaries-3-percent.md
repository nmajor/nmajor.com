# Congressional Research Service — 2 years testing AI on bill summaries, <3% of output usable

FETCHED: 2026-07-20

## Organisation size
Approximately **400 policy analysts and attorneys** (Legis1); CRS total staff ~600. **This is genuinely mid-market sized** — the only candidate in this sweep that is.

## Verbatim — FedScoop, 26 June 2026

> The Congressional Research Service has been testing artificial intelligence on bill summaries in an attempt to address its backlog, but less than 3% of the results met its standards, its director said Thursday.

> To improve it, Director Karen Donfried told the House Administration Committee during a hearing that the service needs a recurring $1.6 million to upgrade to more specialized and confidential models, as well as to staff five data scientists and AI developers.

> "AI is advancing rapidly, and CRS sees its potential to streamline our workflow and enhance our service to Congress," she said in the hearing. "The use of AI, however, also carries risks of outdated information, hallucinations, bias and distortions."

> CRS tested six models over two years on about 1,000 bills, but the vast majority did not meet its standards for accuracy, coherence, relevance and objectivity, Donfried said. The money would help the service upgrade to a model where it could possibly train it on legislative data and input confidential information, she added.

> The lawmakers were "stunned" to hear of the low reliability of AI, with Rep. Julie Johnson, D-Texas, saying, "I think it really highlights that we, as policymakers, can't have a false sense of security of the accuracy of AI, and we have to really put guardrails on that and be vigilant about the data sourcing into these models."

## Real numbers
- **6 large language models** tested, over **2 years** (fiscal 2023 AIWG onward).
- **~1,000 bills** in, **3,000 AI-generated summaries** out.
- **<3% met standard** → **97% rejected** against accuracy, coherence, relevance, objectivity.
- Ask to fix it: **$1.6m recurring**, plus **5 data scientists / AI developers**.
- Next step: a **12-month evaluation in FY2027** comparing ChatGPT, Claude, Google AI, Perplexity, Copilot.
- Org: ~400 policy analysts and attorneys.

## Why this one is valuable
- The workflow — **summarising dense documents** — is precisely the use case every mid-market firm believes is a solved problem. This is a rigorous, adversarially-reviewed, two-year measurement saying it is not, at the quality bar of a professional research organisation.
- The failure mode is *not* "the tech is bad": it's that the job was mis-specified. Donfried's own diagnosis: "writing a bill summary is more complicated than we might think at first blush, because you're not actually summarizing the [text]". The AI summarised the document; the job was to summarise the *change to the law*. That's the essay-shaped insight.
- "Really good at summarizing bills to rename a post office. But when you got to any level of complexity, it was really challenging" is a quotable line about the trivial-vs-real-work gap.

## Caveats — IMPORTANT, do not overstate
- CRS did **not** abandon AI. It is asking for MORE money to try again with better models, and is planning a further 12-month evaluation. This is an honest failed evaluation, **not** a scrapped production deployment. Framing it as "CRS scrapped its AI" would be wrong.
- The <3% was measured on summaries produced by general-purpose models without legislative-data fine-tuning or confidential-data access — which is exactly what CRS says the $1.6m would fix. Include that caveat or the number is misleading.
- "3% accuracy rate" in the oral testimony is looser phrasing than the written "less than 3% of 3000 summaries were acceptable" against a four-part standard. Use the written formulation.
