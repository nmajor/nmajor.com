The most-shared warning about building your own software was written by an agency that sells building your own software.

I went looking for one specific thing. A company roughly your size that built its own tool with AI, and an honest account of what it cost a year later.

Not the launch post. The bill.

I couldn't find one.

The piece everyone points to is a post-mortem on a custom support-triage tool that replaced a $490-a-month Intercom plan, where the six-month cost came out higher than the subscription. It gets shared constantly, and fairly, because it's the only thing anyone cites that puts a number on the part everybody skips.

It's published on a site called Insider AI Trends. The About page says the site has "no vendor deals, just an interest in what's working."

Every byline on it belongs to staff at Kreante, a low-code and AI development agency. The footer on every page reads "AI app development by Kreante."

Then the post opens: "Six months ago, we documented what looked like a clean win."

There is no such post. The only earlier piece on that site about Intercom quotes completely different numbers, and its URL now returns a 404.

It sits inside a series. Replace Mixpanel. Replace Klaviyo. Replace BambooHR. And the sibling post argues that your build method decides "whether the replacement holds for years or breaks in 6 months."

So the definitive cautionary tale about building it yourself was written by people who sell building it yourself, properly, with them.

This isn't one bad website. I went looking for any company in the mid-market that had publicly documented abandoning a tool it built. I found none. Every failure I could actually verify surfaced because something forced it into the open: a parliamentary committee that could compel a chief executive to testify, congressional testimony, a union arbitration, a regulator's disclosure deadline.

Committees, arbitrators, regulators. If you have none of those, nothing makes you publish the bad year, and nobody does.

I'm not accusing anyone of lying. Agencies are allowed to publish marketing. The problem is that this one is load-bearing. It's doing the work of the evidence base for an entire category of decision, and there's nothing underneath it.

We all check the vendor's success story. Almost nobody checks who published the warning.

The version of this that costs you nothing: before a case study changes a decision, spend two minutes on who published it, who profits if it's true, and whether there's a cost line dated more than twelve months after the build. On the piece above, all three come back wrong.

When you last changed a plan because of a case study, do you know who published it?
