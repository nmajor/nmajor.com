J.P. Morgan's payment automation includes a robot that opens envelopes and removes staples.

The bank processed more than 130 million checks in 2025. Those checks arrive with payment stubs, invoices and other paperwork that has to become a usable payment record. Before software can read the documents, something has to unfold them and get the staples out.

This is lockbox processing. Instead of mailing a payment to a company's office, the payer sends it to a bank-managed address. The bank deposits the check and digitizes the accompanying documents so the company can reconcile the payment.

In 2025, [J.P. Morgan Payments deployed a mail automation robot at one lockbox site](https://www.jpmorgan.com/payments/newsroom/ai-robotics-lockbox-processing), developed with document-intelligence company Ripcord. The robot opens envelopes, removes and unfolds the contents, separates pages, removes staples, tracks the contents and scans the documents.

That's a substantial amount of engineering for an envelope.

The bank says the robots can handle more than 4,000 envelope and document permutations. Different layouts and contents create work that a person deals with almost without thinking, but that a machine has to be built to handle. The public account describes the first deployment at one site; it doesn't establish that every lockbox site has the same equipment.

## The system behind the scanner

The robot feeds a processing platform that J.P. Morgan rebuilt in 2020. That system uses AI for data extraction, quality validation and review. The bank later added language models for more complex review tasks.

That sequence connects the physical work to the document work. An opened envelope still leaves someone to identify the payment, read the accompanying information and check whether it is usable. The bank had been improving those processing stages before introducing the mailroom robot.

J.P. Morgan says [data capture that once required roughly 13 billion keystrokes each year is now largely automated](https://www.jpmorgan.com/payments/newsroom/ai-robotics-lockbox-processing). That is a claim about the wider processing operation. It can't be credited to one robot or to the language models alone.

The bank cites its own internal data and doesn't publish enough measurement detail to independently check the result. It also doesn't give a cost saving for the mailroom robot. The public account supports the workflow and the reported reduction in manual entry, rather than a calculation of what this installation returned.

Operators have visibility into throughput, exceptions and turnaround times. They also have an AI assistant for process questions, while the bank describes people handling work that requires judgment.

The deployment reaches from the sealed envelope into the payment-processing system. J.P. Morgan built the physical intake to feed the document software it already had. The staples were part of that work.

A court filing hid instructions for an AI reader in white text.

In Elliott v. New York Bariatric Group, an August 6 Connecticut decision describes tiny white-on-white messages aimed at steering an AI review toward the plaintiff's position. Dechert revisited the case on September 30.

The attempt failed. The court wasn't using AI to decide the motion, and the judge read a printed copy. The hidden instructions had no effect on the ruling.

After repeated concealed messages, including after a warning, the judge revoked the plaintiff's electronic-filing privileges. The order still allowed AI-assisted drafting if the filer independently checked the work.

This is a different failure mechanism from invented citations. A document can carry instructions intended for the software reading it, while looking ordinary to a person.

The sender controlled the document. They were trying to control its reviewer, too.
