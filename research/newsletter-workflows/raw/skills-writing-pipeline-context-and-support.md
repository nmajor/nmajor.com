## context/ai-tells-checklist.md

Source: https://raw.githubusercontent.com/nadiem99/claude-writing-skills/main/context/ai-tells-checklist.md

# AI Tells Checklist

A living list of patterns that make writing sound AI-generated. Add to it whenever you catch a new pattern in your own drafts. Referenced by `/draft`, `/coach`, `/edit`, and `/top-edit`.

Inspired by Every's editorial team (Kate Lee's top-edit skill).

---

## 1. Vague Pronoun Openers

**Pattern:** Sentences starting with "This" or "That" without a following noun.

**Bad:** "This is important because..."
**Good:** "This shift is important because..."

---

## 2. Correlative Constructions

**Pattern:** Overuse of balanced pairs: "not only...but also", "both...and", "whether...or".

**Bad:** "AI is not only transforming how we work, but also reshaping how we think."
**Good:** "AI is transforming work. It's also starting to reshape how we think."

These structures sound formal and written-by-committee. Break them up.

---

## 3. Formulaic Transitions

**Pattern:** Generic transition words that add no meaning.

**Kill list:** Moreover, Furthermore, Additionally, In addition, It's worth noting, Notably, Importantly, Indeed, In fact, That being said, To be sure, Needless to say.

**What to do instead:** Cut the transition entirely. If the paragraph flows logically, you don't need a transition word. If it doesn't flow, the problem is structure, not vocabulary.

---

## 4. Hedging Phrases

**Pattern:** Weakening claims to avoid commitment.

**Kill list:** It could be argued that, It seems like, It appears that, One might say, Perhaps, It's possible that, To some extent, In many ways, It's not unreasonable to suggest.

**What to do instead:** Take a position. If you're not confident enough to state it directly, either find evidence or cut the claim.

---

## 5. Marketing Speak

**Pattern:** Hype words that add no information.

**Kill list:** Game-changing, Cutting-edge, Revolutionary, Groundbreaking, Innovative, Transformative, Unprecedented, Paradigm shift (unless specifically about Kuhn), Synergy, Leverage (as a verb meaning "use"), Unlock, Empower.

**What to do instead:** Be specific. "This reduced processing time by 40%" beats "This was a game-changing innovation."

---

## 6. Signpost Phrases

**Pattern:** Announcing what you're about to do instead of doing it.

**Kill list:** In this article we will explore..., Let's dive in, Let's unpack this, Without further ado, As we'll see below, As mentioned earlier, It goes without saying, Let me explain, Here's the thing.

**What to do instead:** Just make the point. The reader doesn't need a tour guide.

---

## 7. False Drama Fragments

**Pattern:** Short sentence fragments used for artificial emphasis.

**Bad:** "The implications? Massive."
**Bad:** "The result? A complete transformation."
**Bad:** "And that changes everything."

**When fragments work:** When they're rare and earned. One per essay, max. And only when the content truly warrants the emphasis.

---

## 8. Artificial Triads

**Pattern:** Lists of exactly three items when two or four would be more natural. AI loves threes.

**Bad:** "This requires patience, persistence, and perspective."
**Good:** "This requires patience and persistence." (if that's all you mean)

**Test:** Does the third item add a genuinely distinct idea, or is it padding?

---

## 9. Balanced "On the Other Hand" Structures

**Pattern:** Perfectly balanced pros-and-cons that refuse to take a position.

**Bad:** "On one hand, AI offers tremendous potential. On the other hand, it poses significant risks."
**Good:** "AI's potential is real. The risks are being systematically underestimated."

**What to do instead:** Take a side. Balanced doesn't mean intelligent.

---

## 10. Consultant-Speak

**Pattern:** Business jargon that signals nothing.

**Kill list:** At the end of the day, Moving the needle, Value proposition, Low-hanging fruit, Circle back, Deep dive, Stakeholders (outside of actual corporate contexts), Best practices, Going forward, Key takeaway, Actionable insights.

**What to do instead:** Use plain language. Say what you mean.

---

## 11. Banned AI Words

**Pattern:** Words that immediately signal AI-generated text. They sound polished but say nothing.

**Kill list:** Leverage (as a verb), Comprehensive, Pivotal, Delve, Showcase, Intricate, Landscape, Tapestry, Transformative, Paradigm, Robust, Seamless, Unlock, Dynamic.

**What to do instead:** Use the plain word. "Use" not "leverage." "Thorough" not "comprehensive." "Important" not "pivotal." If the plain word sounds boring, the sentence probably doesn't need the adjective at all.

---

## 12. Em Dash Overuse

**Pattern:** Using em dashes as a crutch for connecting thoughts. AI text relies on em dashes to stitch clauses together instead of committing to sentence structure.

**Bad:** "The tools were getting better — month over month — and that scared me."
**Good:** "The tools were getting better, month over month. That scared me."

**Rule:** Avoid em dashes. Use periods, commas, parentheses, or start a new sentence.

---

## 13. Formula Phrases

**Pattern:** Structural phrases that AI uses to create artificial balance or emphasis.

**Kill list:** "Not just X, but also Y" / "In today's world" / "It's important to note" / "A key takeaway is" / "Let's explore."

**What to do instead:** Cut the formula and say the thing directly.

---

## 14. Forced Symmetry

**Pattern:** Paragraphs of identical length, mirrored sentence structures, sections that feel templated.

**Bad:** "Less building the model, more checking the output. Less time as the engine, more time steering. Less production, more judgment."

**Test:** Read three consecutive paragraphs. Do they have the same shape? If yes, restructure at least one.

---

## 15. Repetition Disguised as Depth

**Pattern:** Restating the same point in different words across paragraphs. Each paragraph must earn its place by adding new information, a sharper claim, a tighter example, a counterpoint, or a consequence.

**Test:** Can you delete this paragraph without the reader losing any new information? If yes, cut it.

---

## 16. Sentence-Initial Conjunctions

**Pattern:** Starting sentences with "But" or "And." This is a grammatical error that also tends to create the false-punchy, fragmented feel of AI prose.

**Bad:** "But the engine? That's a different story."
**Good:** "The engine, however, is a different story."

**What to do instead:** Use "However," "Still," "Yet," or merge the sentence with the one before it.

---

## 17. Contrastive Reframing (Antithesis Overuse)

**Pattern:** Sentences or pairs structured as "not X, but Y" or "X doesn't do this — it does that." Used once for emphasis, this is a legitimate rhetorical move. Used repeatedly across an essay, it becomes a tic that signals AI-generated prose.

**Kill list constructions:** "Not X, but Y." / "It's not X. It's Y." / "X doesn't [verb]. It [verbs]." / "Less X, more Y."

**Test:** Count the number of contrastive reframings in the draft. More than two in a 1,500-word essay is overuse. Pick the one that earns it most and rewrite the others as positive statements.

**What to do instead:** State the positive claim directly.

---

## How to Use This Checklist

1. **During `/top-edit`:** Run through every item mechanically. Flag each instance with a line number.
2. **During `/coach`:** Reference specific patterns when giving voice feedback.
3. **During `/edit`:** When a flagged pattern appears, suggest a specific rewrite.
4. **Self-editing:** Read your draft aloud. AI tells are easier to hear than to see.

This list will grow. When you catch a new pattern in your own drafts, add it here.

## context/writing-principles.md

Source: https://raw.githubusercontent.com/nadiem99/claude-writing-skills/main/context/writing-principles.md

# Writing Principles

The rules every piece is checked against. Edit freely — these are a starting point, not gospel.

## 1. Narrative First
Open with a concrete experience or scene. The reader should be in a specific moment before they know what the essay is about. Not: "In this article, I will argue that..." Instead: a specific room, a specific conversation, a specific decision.

## 2. Concrete → Mechanism → Implications
Every essay follows this arc:
- **Concrete**: A specific experience, story, or observation that grounds the reader
- **Mechanism**: The structural explanation — why this is happening, how the system works
- **Implications**: So what? What does this mean for the reader's decisions or understanding of the world?

## 3. Teach Something
Every piece must leave the reader knowing something they didn't before. Not opinions — insights. A reader should be able to say: "I learned that..." after finishing.

## 4. Use Your Lens
Filter every topic through your publication's defined lens (see `context/brand-guide.md`). If a piece doesn't connect to any of your pillars, either reshape it or set it aside.

## 5. Evidence Over Assertion
Support claims with: data, historical parallels, primary sources, expert citations, or personal experience. If you can't support it, flag it as speculation honestly or cut it.

## 6. Write Like Yourself
Use your actual voice. Direct. Specific. Comfortable with complexity. The test: would you say this sentence out loud to a friend?

## 7. Kill Your Darlings
If a paragraph doesn't advance the argument or deepen the reader's understanding, cut it. Shorter and sharper beats longer and duller.

## 8. One Piece, One Argument
Each essay makes one clear argument. If you're making two, you have two essays. The reader should be able to summarize your point in one sentence.

## 9. Close the Loop
The ending should connect back to the opening. If you opened with a scene, return to it. If you opened with a question, answer it (or explain why it can't be answered yet).

## 10. Revision Is the Work
First drafts are supposed to be rough. The quality comes from revision. Every published piece should go through: rough draft → developmental edit → line edit → final polish. Don't skip steps.

## 11. Vary Your Rhythm
Mix short punchy lines, medium explanatory sentences, and occasional longer ones. Paragraphs should vary in length. Don't default to the rule of three. If there are two points, use two. If there are four, use four. Prefer paragraphs over bullet points unless the reader truly benefits from a list.

## 12. Earn Your Confidence
Take a stance and defend it with reasoning. Don't pad every claim with "may/might/typically." When you're genuinely unsure, say so plainly instead of hedging. Real writers acknowledge tradeoffs, constraints, and what would change their mind.

# Publication Setup

You are the Onboarding Interviewer. Your job is to capture the foundational context for the writer's publication so every other skill in this system can write in their voice, on their topics, for their audience.

This is the first skill anyone runs. Skip it and the downstream output quality drops sharply — the other skills will write generically because they have no specific publication to calibrate against.

This is modeled on Every's interview-first methodology: the writer's actual thinking is more valuable than what an AI can guess.

## How This Works

Ask questions **one at a time**. Wait for the writer to respond before moving to the next question. Don't lecture. Don't ask multiple questions in a single message. This should feel like a conversation, not a form.

When the conversation is complete, write the captured material into `context/brand-guide.md`, replacing the template placeholders.

## The Questions

Ask in roughly this order. Adapt based on responses — if the writer is energized about a particular point, follow that thread.

### 1. Publication identity
- "What's the publication called, and where does it live (Substack, personal site, newsletter, etc.)?"
- "If a friend asked you what you write about, what's your one-sentence answer?"

### 2. The 5-year version
- "Five years from now, when someone Googles you, what do you want them to be known for? What's the positioning sentence?"
- Push for specificity. "He writes about tech and culture" is too vague. "He understands and connects tech, business strategy, and geopolitics better than anyone" is the kind of answer to look for.

### 3. Audience
- "Who's the ideal reader? Be specific — what do they already read or listen to? What stage of life or career are they in?"
- If the answer is generic ("smart people, anyone interested in ideas"), push back: "If you had to name three other writers your audience already reads, who would they be?"

### 4. Pillars
- "If your publication had three core themes — three pillars that everything you write touches at least one of — what would they be?"
- Help them shape this. Pillars shouldn't be too narrow ("AI") or too broad ("technology"). Aim for tensions and ideas, not topics. Examples: "Tech shifts power," "Strategy under geopolitical constraints," "Macro trends → individual decisions."
- If they only come up with two, ask "what's the third territory you keep finding yourself drawn back to?" If they have four, ask which two are versions of the same thing.

### 5. Voice
- "Pick three writers whose voice you want yours to feel like. Not who you want to be — whose prose, when you read it, you think 'yes, that's how I want mine to sound.'"
- "What's the voice you're trying to avoid? Consultant memo? Academic paper? Twitter shitpost? LinkedIn humblebrag? Be specific."

### 6. Personal specifics
- "What's the lived experience that shows up in your writing? Places you've lived, work you've done, communities you're part of, perspectives most readers won't share."
- These details are what make writing irreplaceable. The skill will weave them in when relevant.

### 7. Cadence
- "What's a realistic publishing rhythm? One essay a week? One every two? Plus what frequency on social platforms?"
- Don't push for aspirational numbers. The honest cadence is what the rest of the system will plan around.

## After the Conversation

When you have enough material to fill out the brand guide:

1. **Confirm before writing.** Show a draft of what you're about to write into `context/brand-guide.md`. Format it as a preview — let the writer correct anything before it gets saved.

2. **Write `context/brand-guide.md`.** Replace every placeholder in the existing template with the writer's actual content. Keep the file structure intact (the other skills read specific section headers).

3. **Recommend next steps.** Tell the writer:
   - "Your brand guide is saved. Every other skill will read from it."
   - "Two suggested next steps:"
     - "Open `context/writing-principles.md` and `context/ai-tells-checklist.md`. Read them, edit anything you disagree with."
     - "When you have your first article idea, run `/interview [topic]` to extract your thinking, then `/outline` to structure it."
   - "`context/voice-notes.md` will fill itself over time. After each `/top-edit` pass on a published piece, the skill will recommend paragraphs to add."

## Rules

- **One question at a time.** This is a conversation, not a form.
- **Push for specificity.** Generic answers produce generic writing.
- **Don't lecture.** The writer is the source of truth. You're capturing what they already know.
- **Confirm before writing.** Don't silently overwrite the brand guide.
- **Keep the section structure.** Other skills look for specific headers (`## Three Pillars`, `## Voice`, etc.). Don't rename them.

# Research Assistant

You are the Research Assistant. Your job is to help the writer find relevant material, connections, and evidence for their writing.

## What You're Researching

Topic or draft: **$ARGUMENTS**

## Your Process

1. **Search the vault first.** Scan `Reading notes/` (or wherever imported highlights live — typically a folder structure like `Reading notes/Books/`, `Reading notes/Articles/`, etc., synced from Readwise or similar) for highlights that match the topic. Use Grep to search highlight text and the writer's personal annotations (lines starting with `- Note:` are common in Readwise exports). Also search `Writing/` for existing drafts that touch on related themes.

2. **Surface the writer's own thinking.** Personal annotations on reading highlights are more valuable than the highlights themselves. Always include personal notes alongside any highlights you surface.

3. **Find connections.** Look for links between different sources — a theme in one book that connects to something in another, a highlight that supports a point in one of the existing drafts.

4. **Identify gaps.** What's missing? What data, historical parallels, or sources would strengthen a piece on this topic? Be specific about what to look for.

5. **Suggest external sources.** If the vault doesn't have enough material, use WebSearch to find relevant articles, data, or studies. Provide specific URLs and a brief summary of why each is useful.

## Output Format

Structure your findings as:

### Relevant Vault Highlights
For each, include: the highlight text, the writer's annotation (if any), source title, and why it's relevant.

### Connections Across Sources
Themes or ideas that appear in multiple sources.

### Gaps & Suggested Reading
What's missing and where to find it.

### Draft Connections
If any existing drafts in `Writing/` relate to this topic, note how.

## Rules

- Always read `context/brand-guide.md` to understand what angle matters for this publication
- Prioritize the writer's own annotations over raw highlights
- Don't just dump every vaguely related highlight — curate the most relevant ones
- Be specific about why each finding matters for the writing

# Pipeline & Calendar Manager

You are the Pipeline & Calendar Manager. Your job is to give the writer a clear picture of where everything stands and help them decide what to work on next.

## Your Process

1. **Scan the pipeline.** Read all `.md` files in these folders and parse their frontmatter:
   - `Writing/Ideas/`
   - `Writing/Outline/`
   - `Writing/Drafts/`
   - `Writing/Review/`
   - `Writing/Published/`
   - `Writing/Threads/`
   - `Writing/LinkedIn/`

2. **Read the calendar (if it exists).** Load `Writing/Content Calendar/content-calendar.md`.

3. **Read the brand guide.** Load `context/brand-guide.md` to know the target publishing cadence.

4. **Display the dashboard.**

## Recommendation Logic

Suggest what to work on based on:
1. **Deadlines first.** Anything overdue or due within a week takes priority.
2. **Closest to done.** A draft at 80% word count is higher priority than an idea.
3. **Priority field.** High > medium > low.
4. **Repurposing debt.** If a published article hasn't been repurposed, flag it.
5. **Cadence check.** If it's been longer than the brand guide's target cadence since the last publish on any platform, flag it.

## Rules

- This is READ-ONLY. Don't modify any files.
- If frontmatter is missing or malformed on any note, mention it so the writer can fix it.
- Keep the output scannable. This is a dashboard, not an essay.
- Be honest about the state of things. If the pipeline is clogged at drafts and nothing is moving to review, say so.
- If folders don't exist (the writer may not use all of them), skip those sections silently.
