Dear reader,

While chatbots may save companies money, they can be vague, unhelpful and even misleading for consumers. In some cases, the advice is completely wrong, and most of us know they are prone to hallucinating.

In Priceline's case, it has Penny, an "AI travel assistant" which it claims is a customer's "personal hotel expert, destination scout, booking support, and help desk – all in one".

For you, this led to some apparently dud advice and a wild goose chase as you tried to get Priceline to honour Penny's promise. Very sensibly, you had taken a screenshot of "her" advice, in which Penny said your hotel reservation was eligible for cancellation and that you could receive a refund of £386.91.

# 'Penny the chatbot lied to me about a refund and now the hotel won't pay'

Ruth Emery

4 min read

Dear Ruth,

I have an issue with Priceline, a travel platform owned by Booking Holdings. It involves an AI chatbot and a conflicting hotel cancellation policy.

On Aug 27, 2025, I booked three nights at the SIS Kata Resort in Thailand from Nov 4-7, 2025 via Priceline. It was a non-refundable booking, and I received a confirmation email from Priceline. It cost £386.91.

On Oct 28, 2025, I had another accommodation option. I was aware my booking was non-refundable, but I tried to contact Priceline to see whether I could get a partial refund and to assess my options. There was no way of contacting Priceline except via its AI chatbot, which it calls Penny.

When I asked whether it was possible to cancel my booking, Penny informed me that, though it was "originally listed as [a] non-refundable booking", I was in fact eligible for a full refund and it would be in my bank account within 10 working days. I was surprised, but I had it in writing, so when it asked whether I wanted to go ahead, I said yes, and I received an email from Priceline confirming my cancellation.

--- (further section of the same article, from Tavily search snippet of same URL) ---

In terms of whether Priceline should foot the bill for Penny's error, companies generally cannot avoid responsibility by simply saying their chatbot "got it wrong". Indeed, regulators increasingly treat AI statements as those made by the business itself.

Chatbots should comply with consumer law in the same way as human customer service agents do, including not misleading customers.

I pressed the company to review your case again. A couple of weeks later, the US-based firm agreed to send you a cheque in the post. However, Penny had said "she" would process the refund into your account (which never appeared). I was also worried the cheque would be in US dollars, so I asked Priceline to pay the refund by bank transfer instead.

This was finally done last month, when you received £363.11, after foreign currency charges. The company also emailed you acknowledging that the chatbot had given you "misinformation" as the reservation was supposed to be non-refundable.

Given that this refund did not reflect the full amount you had paid for your Thai hotel and that you had waited almost six months for it, I requested an additional gesture. However, I never received an answer to my request, and instead, Priceline said: "We regret the delay and frustration this caused, and we apologise for the inconvenience."

You say you're happy with the refund, even if it's a bit smaller than the full price you paid. This situation highlights the importance of always saving screenshots of "advice" from chatbots and of looking for travel companies that offer other forms of customer service, such as by phone or email. I think I'll also avoid Priceline for any holiday bookings in future.
