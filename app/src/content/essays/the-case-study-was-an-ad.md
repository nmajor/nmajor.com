I went looking for one thing: a company roughly your size that built its own tool with AI, and an honest account of what it cost a year later. Not the launch post. The bill.

I couldn't find one.

The most-quoted cautionary tale in this whole category is a post about a custom support-triage tool that replaced a $490-a-month Intercom subscription, and whose real six-month cost came out higher than the subscription. It gets shared constantly, and for good reason. It's the only piece of writing anyone points to that puts a number on the part everybody skips.

It's published on a site called Insider AI Trends. Every byline on it belongs to staff at Kreante, a low-code and AI development agency. The footer on every page says "AI app development by Kreante." The About page says the site has "no vendor deals, just an interest in what's working."

The post opens: "Six months ago, we documented what looked like a clean win." There is no such post. The only earlier piece on that site about Intercom quotes completely different numbers, $74 a seat and $15 to $40 a month, and its URL now returns a 404. I searched Hacker News across every tag with no date limit and got zero results for the domain. It sits inside a programmatic series with a post for replacing Mixpanel, one for Klaviyo, one for BambooHR. Its sibling post argues that your build method decides "whether the replacement holds for years or breaks in 6 months."

So the definitive failure story about building it yourself was written by people who sell building it yourself, properly, with them.

## Then I checked the wins

A public school district in Washington got a lot of attention, including from me. I [linked it in June](https://www.k12dive.com/news/vibe-coding-helped-a-washington-district-save-250k-in-ed-tech-costs/816993/) as up to $250,000 in software contracts saved by staff who don't write code.

The number moves depending on where you read it. K12 Dive reported $250k in April. An Education Week piece in May had $220k. By late May the district's own Kris Hagel was back at $250k. And [in an April interview](https://www.cionews.com/post/diy-ai-platform-edtech-kris-hagel-peninsula-school-district) there's no dollar figure at all, plus a detail that undercuts the arithmetic: the Claude Code spend is about $100 a month, where earlier tellings said $200.

Hagel's own follow-up lands somewhere honest. "The dollars were never the point," he wrote. "The precedent was."

I'd agree, and I'd add the thing nobody put next to it. Over roughly the same stretch, that board approved a $4.8 million four-year Apple lease. The saving everyone wrote about is around 4.6% of one hardware contract.

## The loop nobody mentions

FranklinCovey is the case I've cited myself. Blaine Carter, the CIO, [told diginomica](https://diginomica.com/buy-build-how-franklincovey-cio-blaine-carter-replacing-packaged-apps-low-code-and-ai) he's replacing packaged software with things his team builds, and the figure that travelled was about $50,000 a year on a finance app.

There's an earlier source. A Make.com customer story from September 2025, six months before the diginomica piece, says $60,000, not $50,000. It's also a different department, and its headline claim is HR workflows going "from 30 days to 2 hours," where diginomica describes a fiscal binder going from 30 days to a few days. Same company, same story, different numbers and different speed.

That vendor page also says what the problem was. FranklinCovey had a 15-year-old in-house service bus whose original developers are long gone.

The in-house build replacing the SaaS is replacing an in-house build that rotted. Carter's most recent appearance, in [MES Computing in April](https://www.mescomputing.com/news/2026/ai/ai-tools-and-platforms-when-midmarket-it-teams-should-build-vs-buy-ai-in-2026), is in a piece arguing agentic AI is impractical for mid-market teams to build in-house.

## What silence looks like

In April, Brivo's COO Hans Kahler posted that he'd built an internal HR objectives tool himself and avoided close to $100,000 in software. A commenter wrote underneath that teams who build their own tools "own the long-term maintenance cost, which compounds over time."

Seven weeks later Kahler announced he was leaving Brivo after 13 years.

Nobody has said the tool broke. Nobody has said anything about it at all. The person who built it is gone, and there is no public record of who has it now. That's not a scandal. It's the normal shape of these stories, which is that they stop.

To be fair, one of them didn't stop. Atonom dropped Salesforce for a CRM built on Lovable in 2025, and nine months later founder Gabe Larsen went on [a podcast](https://gtmhackers.com/episodes/he-ripped-out-salesforce-to-vibe-code-his-own-crm-autonomous-organization) and said the maintenance worry is overblown. It's still running. That cuts against my argument, so there it is.

## Why the bill is always missing

I looked hard for a mid-market company that had publicly documented abandoning an AI tool it built. I found none.

Every failure I could verify surfaced because something forced it out. A UK outsourcer's £239 million government pensions contract, priced on an assumption that 95% of transactions would be automated, came apart in front of a parliamentary committee that could compel the chief executive to testify. A government research office found under 3% of its AI-generated bill summaries met standard, and that came out in congressional testimony. The model summarised the document, when the job was to summarise the change to the law.

Committees, arbitrators, regulators. If you have none of those, you have no reason to publish, and nobody does.

I've cited two of these stories myself, in this newsletter, as evidence that building your own was working. Neither of them has a number in it dated after year one. Neither will most of what you read this quarter.

So how would any of us actually know whether building your own pays, when the only people holding the numbers have no reason to show them? And if the answer is that you find out from your own books eighteen months in, what would you need to be writing down now?
