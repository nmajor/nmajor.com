# NEGATIVE FINDING — companies do not disclose AI write-offs in SEC filings, and the reason is legal

FETCHED: 2026-07-20
STATUS: This file records a **null result** from the "earnings call / 10-K / 10-Q impairment or discontinued AI initiative" search angle, plus the evidence explaining *why* it is null. The null result is itself the finding.

## What was searched
Multiple queries against SEC EDGAR content, 10-K/10-Q text, and earnings-call coverage for "impairment", "write-off", "discontinued" co-occurring with AI initiatives, 2025-06-01 onward.

## What came back
Nothing on point. Every impairment hit was either (a) unrelated to AI (Marvell restructuring, Adeia goodwill, Lockheed), or (b) an AI *vendor's* own losses rather than a customer abandoning a deployment (C3.ai). **No company was found disclosing an impairment or write-off attributable to abandoning an internal AI deployment.**

Notably, C3.ai's FY2026 10-K states: "There were no impairment charges recognized related to long-lived assets during the fiscal years ended April 30, 2026, 2025 and 2024."

## The evidence for WHY this angle is empty

SOURCE: https://www.cfodive.com/news/few-10ks-tie-ai-tangible-revenue-gains-cybersecurity/812917 (CFO Dive — "Few 10-Ks tie AI to tangible revenue gains, study finds")

> Eighty-five percent of Fortune 500 companies now mention artificial intelligence in their annual 10-K filings with the Securities and Exchange Commission, up from 29% in 2022... Yet the technology remains an "operational tool" by and large and is not yet a primary revenue driver for most organizations using it.

> Rios said he uncovered more than 20 instances of Fortune 500 companies hedging anticipated AI investment outcomes with "no-assurance" language. In one example, fintech company Fiserv said **"there can be no assurance that our use of artificial intelligence will enhance our products or services or achieve any improvements in innovation or efficiency."**

> "CFOs seem to be in a double bind where they're compelled to invest in AI to stay competitive, yet they're having to use very cautious language" when disclosing these initiatives, Rios said. **"If you claim 20% cost savings and it doesn't hold up, that could be a shareholder lawsuit."**

> Almost all of the companies touting concrete AI investment returns in 10-K filings are major infrastructure sellers in the space such as NVIDIA, Broadcom and Micron, Rios said. Most of the other organizations use vague language around AI "productivity" and "efficiency" with almost no quantified impact.

Supporting: PwC survey via same article —

## The takeaway worth using
The structural reason there is no public record of enterprise AI write-offs: **internal AI deployments are almost never capitalised as a separately-identified asset**, so there is nothing to impair. Spend runs through opex — cloud, tokens, headcount, consulting — and vanishes into SG&A. Combined with counsel advising "no-assurance" hedging precisely so that an unmet claim cannot become a shareholder suit, the disclosure system is built so that abandoning an AI project leaves **no financial footprint at all**.

That is why the failures that do surface come from somewhere else entirely: a **union arbitration** (POLITICO), a **labour tribunal** (Commonwealth Bank), a **parliamentary select committee** (Capita), a **congressional budget hearing** (CRS), or a **regulatory compliance deadline** (US federal agency AI inventories). Every documented failure in this sweep surfaced because an *external accountability mechanism with subpoena or disclosure power* forced it into the open — never because a company volunteered it.

Corollary for anyone citing failure rates: the widely-recycled "95% of AI pilots fail" (MIT NANDA) and "80% fail" (RAND) numbers are doing so much work in public discourse precisely *because* the bottom-up, company-by-company evidence base does not exist. Treat the surveys sceptically, but note that the absence of counter-evidence is a disclosure artifact, not proof things are going well.
