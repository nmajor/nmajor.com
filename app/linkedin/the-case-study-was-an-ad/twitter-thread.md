1/ I went looking for one mid-market company that built its own tool with AI and published what it cost a year later.

Not the launch post. The bill.

I couldn't find one. Here's what I found instead.

2/ The most-quoted cautionary tale in the category: a custom support-triage tool that replaced a $490-a-month Intercom subscription, whose real six-month cost came out higher.

It's the only piece anyone points to that puts a number on the part everybody skips.

3/ It's on a site called Insider AI Trends. Every byline belongs to staff at Kreante, a low-code and AI development agency.

The footer on every page says "AI app development by Kreante." The About page says the site has "no vendor deals."

4/ The post opens "Six months ago, we documented what looked like a clean win."

There is no such post.

The only earlier Intercom piece on the site quotes different numbers ($74 a seat, $15 to $40 a month) and its URL now 404s.

5/ It sits in a programmatic series. One post for Mixpanel, one for Klaviyo, one for BambooHR.

A sibling post argues your build method decides whether it "holds for years or breaks in 6 months."

The failure story was written by people who sell doing it properly.

6/ So I checked the wins. Including two I'd cited myself.

A Washington school district's savings: $250k in K12 Dive in April. $220k in Education Week in May. Back to $250k late May. An April interview has no dollar figure at all.

7/ That same interview puts the district's Claude Code spend at about $100 a month, where earlier tellings said $200.

Their own Kris Hagel landed somewhere honest: "The dollars were never the point. The precedent was."

8/ I'd agree. I'd add the thing nobody put next to it.

Over roughly the same stretch, that board approved a $4.8 million four-year Apple lease.

The saving everyone wrote about is around 4.6% of one hardware contract.

9/ FranklinCovey: about $50,000 a year on a finance app, per diginomica.

An earlier Make.com customer story says $60,000. Different department. "30 days to 2 hours," where diginomica describes 30 days to a few days.

Same company, same story, different numbers.

10/ That vendor page also says what was being replaced: a 15-year-old in-house service bus whose original developers are long gone.

The in-house build replacing the SaaS is replacing an in-house build that rotted.

11/ In April, Brivo's COO posted that he'd built an internal HR objectives tool himself and avoided close to $100,000 in software.

A commenter warned that teams who build their own "own the long-term maintenance cost, which compounds over time."

12/ Seven weeks later he announced he was leaving Brivo after 13 years.

Nobody has said the tool broke. Nobody has said anything about it at all.

That's not a scandal. It's the normal shape of these stories, which is that they stop.

13/ One cuts against me, so here it is.

Atonom dropped Salesforce for a CRM built on Lovable in 2025. Nine months later the founder said the maintenance worry is overblown.

It's still running.

14/ Every failure I could verify surfaced because something forced it out.

A £239m UK government pensions contract, priced on 95% of transactions being automated, came apart in front of a parliamentary committee that could compel the CEO to testify.

15/ A government research office found under 3% of its AI-generated bill summaries met standard. That came out in congressional testimony.

The model summarised the document, when the job was to summarise the change to the law.

16/ Committees, arbitrators, regulators.

If you have none of those, you have no reason to publish. And nobody does.

I've cited two of these stories in my own newsletter as evidence building your own was working. Neither has a number dated after year one.

17/ How would any of us know whether building your own pays, when the only people holding the numbers have no reason to show them?
