Kinney Drugs (a pharmacy chain in NY and Vermont) ran an AI phone assistant for all incoming calls for 3 months before killing it and replacing it with something more sensible.

Patients had reported [incoherent calls, wrong medication dosages, and missed prescription notifications](https://www.wcax.com/2026/08/07/kinney-drugs-pulls-back-ai-phone-assistant-after-hundreds-customer-complaints/). The chain cut their losses and put incoming calls back on a touch-tone system.

Their AI phone assistant kept one job. Outbound refill texts, to patients who opted in.

Compare the two jobs. Incoming calls, full conversation mode. It has to understand and respond and get every detail correct. Vs outbound texts. The patient asked to receive them, and the worst case is a reminder they didn't need.

It's not hard to find companies who have "AI'd all the things" only to roll things back to something more sensible.

Johnson & Johnson ran what its CIO Jim Swanson calls a "thousand flowers" approach and got to [nearly 9,000 AI use cases](https://www.tbpndigest.com/story/2025-04-18/johnson-johnson-cuts-9000-ai-projects-down-to-its-highest-value-use-cases-after-a-year-of-experimentation). When they went back and measured, 10 to 15% of those use cases were producing 80% of the value. So they cut to four areas: a co-pilot for sales reps, drug discovery, supply chain risk, and an internal HR chatbot that handles about 10 million employee interactions a year.

Amazon pulled Just Walk Out from its Fresh supermarkets in 2024 and [put smart trolleys in instead](https://www.npr.org/2024/04/03/1242508931/no-more-just-walk-out-at-amazon-grocery-stores-the-new-bet-is-smart-shopping-car). Then it went further and [closed all 72 of its own Fresh and Go stores](https://www.geekwire.com/2026/amazon-closing-all-amazon-fresh-and-go-stores-to-focus-on-whole-foods-and-grocery-delivery/) by February this year. The technology is still running, just not in Amazon's own shops. It's licensed out to more than 360 other locations, stadiums and hospitals among them, plus 40-odd breakrooms in Amazon's warehouses. A weekly shop with 90 items was too much. Three items and a sandwich was fine.

Taco Bell put voice AI in hundreds of US restaurants. Then came the accuracy complaints, and a viral order for 18,000 cups of water. In August 2025 its chief digital officer said busier restaurants ["may benefit more from a human taking orders"](https://www.nrn.com/restaurant-technology/taco-bell-is-adjusting-its-voice-ai-plans), and the rollout slowed right down. It's now in [more than 890 restaurants across 38 states](https://www.restaurantdive.com/news/taco-bell-omilia-drive-thru-ai-deployment/824564/). The slowdown bought them the information. They found out what it got wrong while that was still cheap, then went nine times wider than before it broke.

I don't think that's stupidity. It's the arithmetic. When you're deciding where to point an AI tool, you look for the biggest number: the most calls, the busiest stores, the widest rollout, because that's where the savings look worth the effort. Any business case built that way lands you on the highest-volume version of the job.

Which is the same thing as the highest-exposure version. High volume means more chances to be wrong per week, and the busiest channel is usually the one your customers can see. So the first deployment gets aimed at the place where errors are most expensive and most public, by a process that felt rigorous at the time.

Then it fails, and the company picks a narrower job. The narrower job usually works, because by then they know exactly which mistakes the thing makes.

The alternative is to go slower. It's less flashy for shareholders, but overestimating AI makes you a 2026 cautionary tale.
