You've seen the stat that 95% of AI projects fail. That isn't what the study measured.

It comes from a report by Project NANDA at the MIT Media Lab, published in 2025.

Around 95% of organisations reported no measurable P&L impact from their generative AI spend. Separately, that only 5% of custom enterprise AI tools reach production.

"No measurable P&L impact" and "failed" are not the same claim. Most internal tooling of any kind has never had a P&L line attached to it. Your ticketing system doesn't have one either.

The methodology, which almost nobody quotes: 153 senior leaders recruited at four conferences, no response rate given, and anonymised company data that nobody can independently check. A sample of people already at AI conferences.

And NANDA builds agent infrastructure. The report's conclusion is that the fix is agentic systems.

There's a number in it I've never once seen quoted. Mid-market organisations moved from pilot to full implementation in about 90 days, against nine months or longer for large enterprises.

That one cuts against the doom framing, so it doesn't travel.

None of this makes the report worthless. Directionally I think it's probably right that most firms can't attach a number to this spend, and that's a real problem worth naming. But it's 153 conference attendees, and I've watched it get used to settle budget arguments as though it were a census.

If you're going to cite it, cite what it found. Most firms could not attach a P&L number to their AI spend. That's a sharper claim than "95% fail," and it survives contact with the source.

It's also the more useful one, because it's a test you can run on your own proposals. For anything AI you're being asked to fund: which operating number is this supposed to move, what is that number today, who owns it, and when do we check. Most of the 95% aren't failures. They're deployments nobody set up to be measured, which is a fixable problem and a different one.

What's a number you've repeated for a while without reading the study behind it?
